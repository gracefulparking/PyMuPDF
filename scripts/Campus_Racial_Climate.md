# Campus Racial Climate
*Scholar Stacks — Long-Form Concept Episode*

**Concept origins:** 1992
**Sources drawn from:** 2 corpus texts
**Historical briefing + lineage map:** included

---

DEV: In November 2015, the president and chancellor of the University of Missouri resigned within four days of each other. The proximate cause was a graduate student's hunger strike and the football team refusing to play. But the deeper cause was something the higher education research community had been measuring, naming, and publishing about for twenty-three years at that point. Sylvia Hurtado's article "The Campus Racial Climate: Contexts of Conflict" came out in 1992. By the time Mizzou happened, the field had four-dimensional models, validated survey instruments, longitudinal data sets, an entire empirical apparatus built around the idea that the racial environment of a campus shapes what happens to the students living inside it. And it still didn't prevent — or even particularly predict — what unfolded that fall.

MAYA: So the puzzle is what, exactly — that we had the language and the measurement and it didn't save us?

DEV: The puzzle is that the framework was vindicated and inadequate at the same time. It had named the dynamics. It had also, in a sense, helped institutions feel like naming was doing.

MAYA: That hits where I live. I'm in the middle of a dissertation chapter on belonging at a regional comprehensive, and I keep running into this — every institution I look at has a climate survey, a climate report, a climate task force. And when I ask Black students what's actually going on, none of that infrastructure is doing the thing the literature said it would do. So I want to understand where this framework came from, what it was built to do, and what it's actually capable of carrying.

[pause]

DEV: Let's start with the moment. 1992. Hurtado publishes in the *Journal of Higher Education*. The same year, *United States v. Fordice* comes down from the Supreme Court — Mississippi's higher ed system ruled to have not dismantled its dual segregated structure. The Rodney King beating happens in '91; the LA uprising in spring '92. Hurtado is at UCLA. William Tierney publishes "Official Encouragement, Institutional Discouragement" in the same journal the same year, arguing that Tinto's model of student integration is assimilationist when applied to minoritized students. The field is, all at once, looking up from the persistence-and-retention literature and asking whether the framework everyone has been using describes what students of color actually experience.

MAYA: Wait — why did they need a new concept? Allen had been doing comparative work on Black students at HBCUs versus PWIs. Fleming had *Blacks in College* in '84. Nettles. There was a "minority student experience" literature already.

DEV: Yes, and that's the productive question. What that earlier literature gave you was descriptive — here's what Black students at PWIs report, here's how it compares to HBCUs. What it didn't give you was an *environmental* framework. A way to say: the institution itself has properties, those properties are measurable, and those properties produce the experiences. The shift Hurtado makes — and she's drawing on Astin's I-E-O model and Rudolf Moos's social climate scales, which both trace back to Kurt Lewin's field theory from the 1930s, behavior is a function of person and environment — the shift is to make the campus environment the object of analysis rather than the backdrop.

MAYA: So the move is methodological as much as conceptual. You're making race-on-campus into something you can put on the left side of a regression.

DEV: That's exactly the move. And the other thing happening in the early '90s is legal. Powell's opinion in *Bakke* in 1978 had preserved race-conscious admissions on the thin reed of a "diversity" interest — but Powell's diversity rationale was, candidly, constitutional dicta with almost no empirical backing. By the early '90s, *Podberesky* strikes down the Banneker Scholarship at Maryland. *Hopwood* is on the horizon in the Fifth Circuit. Prop 209 is being organized in California. The legal scaffolding around race-conscious policy is visibly cracking. And inside the academy, there's a small number of researchers — Hurtado, Patricia Gurin at Michigan, Mitchell Chang as a grad student, eventually Jeffrey Milem, Anna Clayton-Pedersen, Walter Allen as a co-author on the 1998 *Review of Higher Education* piece — who are building what will become the empirical case that diversity isn't just constitutionally permitted, it's educationally necessary.

MAYA: So the framework is born partly as a defensive instrument. That's not a knock — but it's a feature of the intellectual DNA.

DEV: It's a feature you can't understand the framework without. Hurtado's 1998 article with Milem, Clayton-Pedersen, and Allen — the four-dimensional model gets fully articulated there, and then the 1999 ASHE-ERIC monograph *Enacting Diverse Learning Environments* makes it a field-wide vocabulary. Five years later, Patricia Gurin's expert report in *Grutter* is the empirical centerpiece of Michigan Law's defense. O'Connor cites it in the majority opinion. The chain from Hurtado's '92 article to O'Connor's 2003 opinion is direct, and the foundations — Ford, Mellon — funded large pieces of it deliberately.

MAYA: Which means scholarship and advocacy are pretty hard to disentangle in this period.

DEV: They are. Both as a strength — real-world stakes, real institutional uptake — and as a constraint on what gets studied. The selective four-year sector is the focus. Community colleges are an early blind spot. For-profits, when they explode in the 2000s, are nearly absent. The framework matures around the institutions whose admissions practices were under legal threat.

[pause]

MAYA: Okay, give me the four dimensions. What does the framework actually claim?

DEV: Four dimensions of campus racial climate, all of them irreducible to each other. One: the institution's *historical legacy of inclusion or exclusion*. Two: *structural diversity* — the compositional representation of different racial and ethnic groups on campus. Three: the *psychological climate* — perceptions, attitudes, sense of racial tension or belonging. Four: the *behavioral climate* — actual intergroup interactions and the quality of those interactions.

MAYA: Let me make this concrete. Pick a campus.

DEV: Take a flagship public research university in the South. Land-grant institution, founded in the 19th century, didn't admit its first Black undergraduate until the early 1960s under federal court order. That's dimension one — the historical legacy is not a vibe, it's a documented sequence of policies. The buildings are named after segregationists. The endowment was built partly on tuition from people who would not have admitted the students currently sitting in the seats. That history doesn't go away because someone wrote a strategic plan in 2015.

MAYA: Right.

DEV: Dimension two, structural diversity. The undergraduate population is 6% Black in a state that's 30% Black. Two percent of tenured faculty are Black. The dorms and the Greek system are visibly stratified by race. That's the compositional layer.

MAYA: And those two interact. The historical legacy partly explains the current numbers.

DEV: They interact, but they don't reduce to each other. That's the framework's insistence. Now dimension three — psychological climate. You survey students. White students rate the campus climate as welcoming and racially comfortable. Black students rate it dramatically lower, report higher levels of perceived racial tension, report lower sense of belonging. The framework refuses to treat that as one campus climate that some students misperceive. It treats it as constitutive of what climate is.

MAYA: That's the Lewin import you mentioned — the environment as phenomenologically constructed.

DEV: That's the Lewin import, and it's the one Hurtado holds onto where a lot of her successors slide off it. Climate is not a property all students experience identically. Dimension four, behavioral. You can count interactions — does cross-racial interaction happen in classes, in residence halls, in cocurricular life? — and you can characterize them. Are they substantive? Are they superficial? Are they hostile?

MAYA: So tracking one student through this — say, a Black sophomore at this university — what does the framework do for me that just asking her about her experience wouldn't?

DEV: It forces you to refuse the explanation that what's happening to her is reducible to one dimension. If she's struggling with belonging, the framework prevents you from saying "well, we just need more Black students" — structural diversity alone — without asking what those students will encounter when they arrive. It prevents you from saying "we'll do a bias training" — psychological climate alone — without addressing the buildings still named for segregationists. It prevents the easy out at every level.

MAYA: And it lets you compare campuses, which is the empirical payoff.

DEV: Right. And here's where the Allport contact hypothesis substrate matters. Gordon Allport in 1954 — intergroup prejudice can be reduced under specific conditions: equal status, common goals, institutional support, cooperative interdependence. The behavioral and psychological dimensions of climate research are doing Allport in cognitive-developmental clothing. The educational-benefits-of-diversity argument that Gurin makes in *Grutter* is essentially that under the right conditions, structural diversity produces the cross-racial contact that produces the cognitive and democratic outcomes the Court should care about.

MAYA: Strategically downplayed in the legal version, though — the preconditions.

DEV: That's the move I'd push on. The legal version of the argument often implies that structural diversity will yield the benefits more or less automatically. Allport never said that. Gurin herself never said that. But the litigation needed a cleaner story than "diversity will work if you build twelve specific kinds of institutional infrastructure," so the preconditions got softened.

MAYA: Which puts a lot of pressure on institutions that admitted diverse classes after *Grutter* and then didn't do the structural work.

DEV: It puts all the pressure there. And it's part of what 2015 exposed. You had two decades of structural diversity gains at selective institutions without commensurate change in the historical legacy or the psychological and behavioral dimensions. The framework predicted that would be insufficient. The framework also got used by institutions to argue they were doing the work because they were measuring it.

[pause]

MAYA: So how does this concept evolve after *Grutter*?

DEV: Two big moves. The first is Samuel Museus and Uma Jayakumar in 2012, the edited volume *Creating Campus Cultures*, and then Museus's CECE model — Culturally Engaging Campus Environments — in the 2014 *Higher Education: Handbook of Theory and Research* chapter. Museus's move is to shift the unit of analysis from climate to culture. Climate is perceptions and interactions. Culture is the deeper symbolic order — values, beliefs, assumptions of the institution. The corpus is explicit on this. Museus and Jayakumar write that "by cultivating values, beliefs, and assumptions that focus on including, validating, and creating equitable outcomes among diverse undergraduate students, an institution can foster their success."

MAYA: That sounds like a soft pivot. What's actually different?

DEV: What's different is the directionality. Tinto's persistence model — which had structured retention research since the 1970s — required students to integrate into the institution's culture. CECE inverts that. The institution has to become engageable by the students. Museus, writing with Yi and Saelua in the 2017 *Review of Higher Education* piece on sense of belonging, says it directly — "traditional student success frameworks, such as Tinto's integration theory, and the research that has employed them to study student success have focused more on students' behaviors than the environmental and psychological elements of college success."

MAYA: So the locus of change moves from the student to the institution.

DEV: That's the inversion. And CECE specifies nine indicators across two categories. Cultural relevance: cultural familiarity, culturally relevant knowledge, cultural community service, cross-cultural engagement, culturally validating environments. Cultural responsiveness: collectivist orientations, humanized environments, proactive philosophies, holistic support.

MAYA: That's a lot of indicators.

DEV: It is, and the proliferation is partly because Museus is trying to give institutional assessment something operationalizable that doesn't collapse back into "did we hire a chief diversity officer." The other thing he's doing is absorbing Tara Yosso's 2005 "Whose Culture Has Capital?" — community cultural wealth. The idea that minoritized students bring aspirational, navigational, social, linguistic, familial, and resistant capital into the institution, and that the deficit framing of much earlier student success research had rendered those resources invisible. CECE tries to build that into the measurement model.

MAYA: So is the original Hurtado framework still usable? Or has it been superseded?

DEV: This is contested, and I'd say honestly: it's been supplemented, not superseded. CECE has uptake among scholars and progressive practitioners. The original four-dimensional Hurtado framework remains the default vocabulary in DEI strategic plans across thousands of institutions. They coexist because they serve different audiences. Where Hurtado wrote about climate as conditions, Museus writes about culture as what the institution actually is. The Museus and Jayakumar volume is candid about this — they note that many institutions exhibit, in their words, "watered down versions of multiculturalism" and have what they call "diversity-oriented" cultures that "may achieve diversity in their student bodies" without achieving equity in outcomes. That's a critique of where the original climate work landed in practice.

MAYA: That feels related to Derrick Bell's interest convergence idea.

DEV: It's explicit. The corpus has Museus and Jayakumar invoking interest convergence directly — institutions embrace diversity when and to the extent it benefits dominant group interests, which is exactly the argument Bell made about *Brown v. Board* in 1980. The CECE move is partly an attempt to specify what an equity-oriented culture would require beyond a diversity-oriented one.

[pause]

MAYA: Where is the concept contested right now? Give me real disagreements, not generic "more research is needed."

DEV: I'll give you three. First — and this is the deepest one — measurement versus structural critique. Climate research and CECE both live by surveys, scales, regressions, institutional assessment. Critical Race Theory scholars and the abolitionist university literature — la paperson's *A Third University Is Possible*, Sharon Stein's *Unsettling the University* — argue that this methodological apparatus commits the research, structurally, to managerial reform of an institution that should be transformed. Lori Patton's 2016 piece on the limits of CRT uptake in student affairs is the cleanest version of this critique inside the field. The charge is that climate research has domesticated CRT — kept the vocabulary, lost the structural analysis.

MAYA: Hold on — I want to push on that, because it can read as a purity critique. If the surveys produce institutional change, even partial, isn't that better than the analytic depth that produces no traction?

DEV: That's the defenders' response and I take it seriously. Hurtado herself has continued working — her recent writing on "equity-minded" frameworks tries to hold both. But the question the critics raise that I can't dismiss: has thirty years of climate measurement produced the structural change the framework was supposed to enable? Or has it produced an industry of measurement that lets institutions report on a problem they are not solving?

MAYA: That's an empirical question, not just a theoretical one.

DEV: It is, and I don't have a clean answer. I'd say the honest read is: climate measurement has produced some changes — staffing, programming, language — and it has not produced the structural shifts in faculty composition, in resource allocation, in curriculum, in governance that would actually move the needle on the dimensions the framework names.

MAYA: Okay, second debate.

DEV: Whether "diversity" is the right frame at all. After *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard* in 2023, the Court explicitly rejected the diversity rationale the climate literature spent thirty years empirically supporting. Lani Guinier and Derrick Bell argued years before that the diversity-as-educational-benefit frame had traded justice-based arguments for utility-based ones and lost both — once you ground the case in the benefits to all students of having minoritized students present, you've made minoritized students an instrument of others' education rather than rights-holders in their own right. *SFFA* arguably proves the strategic vulnerability of that frame.

MAYA: And the field is now reorganizing without its central legal anchor.

DEV: Without its central legal anchor and, in many states, without its institutional infrastructure. Florida SB 266, Texas SB 17 — the dismantling of named DEI offices. The framework hasn't disappeared. It's being reorganized, rebranded, sometimes hollowed out.

MAYA: Third debate.

DEV: Whether the framework's racial categories carry the load they need to carry. The original climate work was built largely around a Black-white axis, with Latinx students added in subsequent waves. After October 7, 2023, the campus protests of the 2023-24 academic year exposed something the framework has struggled with — Jewish students, Palestinian and Arab and Muslim students, students whose experiences don't sort cleanly onto the racial categories the framework grew up using. The framework was also slow to incorporate post-9/11 surveillance of Muslim and South Asian students. There's a real question about whether the categories themselves are robust to the conflicts campuses are actually living through.

MAYA: I want to push back here too. The categories aren't broken just because a new conflict surfaces. The framework's claim was always that historical legacy, structural composition, perceptions, and interactions matter — those are general categories. The specific groups they apply to can expand.

DEV: That's fair. I'd partly accept that. The structural categories are extensible. But the operationalization — the specific scales, the specific items, the specific comparisons that have been done at scale — was built around particular axes, and there's a lag between the framework's theoretical extensibility and the empirical infrastructure catching up.

[pause]

MAYA: What does the field routinely get wrong about this concept?

DEV: The biggest flattening is structural diversity. When practitioners and administrators use the term "campus climate," nine times out of ten they mean the composition of the student body and a survey of attitudes. Two of the four dimensions, treated as if they were the whole thing. The historical legacy gets reduced to a land acknowledgment. The behavioral dimension gets reduced to programming counts — how many dialogues did we host. And the interaction between dimensions, which is the framework's actual analytic contribution, gets dropped entirely.

MAYA: So institutions report on climate as if it were one number.

DEV: Or two numbers. Demographic share and satisfaction. And the whole point of the four-dimensional model was to insist that a campus can be racially diverse on paper, score adequately on a climate survey, and still be carrying a historical legacy and a set of behavioral patterns that make it hostile in ways the top-line numbers won't show. Hurtado built the framework against the easy reading. The easy reading won anyway in most institutional uptake.

MAYA: That tracks with what I see. Climate reports that read like marketing.

DEV: And the other flattening — Lewin's phenomenological point. Climate is not a single property of the campus. It's experienced differently by students differently positioned in it. When you average all student responses into a campus climate score, you're erasing the thing you're supposed to be measuring.

[pause]

MAYA: Implications for the people listening. Let me work through this. If you're a doctoral student studying belonging, persistence, or outcomes for minoritized students — what does this framework tell you to do?

DEV: It tells you not to study climate as one variable. If you're putting climate on the right side of a regression predicting GPA or persistence, you're already doing it wrong. The framework requires you to disaggregate. Historical legacy is an institutional-level variable that doesn't vary across students at the same campus. Structural diversity is also institutional-level but interacts with the student's own racial position. Psychological climate has to be measured group-by-group, not averaged. Behavioral climate needs observation, not just self-report.

MAYA: And if I'm a practitioner — a residence life director, an academic advisor, a DEI staff person — what changes Monday morning?

DEV: Stop reading the climate survey as a thermometer reading. Read it as four separate diagnostic instruments and ask which dimensions your institution is actually trying to move. If your only intervention is programming — bias training, dialogues — you're working on the behavioral and psychological dimensions while leaving the historical legacy and the structural composition untouched. That's not a strategy. That's the path of least resistance, and the framework, properly understood, predicts it won't work.

MAYA: For faculty.

DEV: For faculty, the implication is curricular and pedagogical. The behavioral dimension of climate is, in significant part, what happens in your classroom. Whether cross-racial discussion happens substantively or superficially. Whether minoritized students are positioned as knowers or as case studies. The Patricia Gurin argument that *Grutter* relied on was that the classroom was the unit where diversity's educational benefits get realized — meaning faculty are climate workers whether they think of themselves that way or not.

MAYA: And for administrators.

DEV: For administrators, the hardest implication, the one most often ducked: the historical legacy dimension is the one with the most institutional teeth and the least frequent intervention. Renaming buildings, repatriating ancestral remains, redirecting resources, governance reform — these are the moves the framework points to as constitutive of climate, and they're the moves institutions are structurally least equipped to make. If you're not working on the historical legacy, you're not working on climate. You're working on the parts of climate that can be addressed with a programming budget.

MAYA: That is going to land hard for some people.

DEV: It should.

[pause]

DEV: Reading roadmap. Four texts, in order. First, Hurtado, Milem, Clayton-Pedersen, and Allen, 1998, *Review of Higher Education*, "Enhancing Campus Climates for Racial/Ethnic Diversity." That's the cleanest articulation of the four-dimensional framework. Start there because every subsequent critique presupposes you've encountered the original framing in its own voice.

MAYA: Second.

DEV: Second, Patricia Gurin, Eric Dey, Sylvia Hurtado, and Gerald Gurin, 2002, *Harvard Educational Review*, "Diversity and Higher Education: Theory and Impact on Educational Outcomes." Read this second because it shows what happened when the climate framework was operationalized into the causal-mechanism argument the legal system needed. You can't understand the framework's institutional power without seeing the *Grutter* version of itself.

MAYA: Third.

DEV: Third, Tara Yosso, 2005, *Race Ethnicity and Education*, "Whose Culture Has Capital?" Read this third because it's the most-cited piece of the critical counter-current and because everything in the CECE line is downstream of it. You need it to understand what the climate literature was missing about minoritized students' resources.

MAYA: Fourth.

DEV: Fourth, Museus 2014, the CECE chapter in the *Higher Education: Handbook of Theory and Research*, and the 2017 *Review of Higher Education* article on sense of belonging with Yi and Saelua. Read these fourth because they show what the field looks like after thirty years of climate research has been absorbed and partially superseded — culturally engaging environments, the inversion of Tinto, the operationalized version of the cultural turn.

[pause]

DEV: What we don't know.

MAYA: Honestly?

DEV: Honestly, we don't know what the framework looks like in the absence of the legal regime it was built to defend. The entire empirical and conceptual apparatus matured around the diversity-as-educational-benefit argument that *Grutter* secured and *SFFA* dismantled. The field has spent thirty years building measurement instruments, training doctoral students, structuring institutional assessment, and accumulating evidence in service of a legal frame that no longer holds. What happens to climate research when its political-legal anchor is gone, when the institutional infrastructure of DEI offices is being dismantled in red states, when the diversity coalition fractures along lines the framework's categories don't cleanly address — we don't know. Hurtado has gestured at "equity-minded" alternatives. The CECE line has tried to build a post-diversity vocabulary. Neither has consolidated. The next move, if it comes, may not come from inside the climate-and-measurement tradition at all. It may come from the abolitionist and decolonial literatures that have been arguing for two decades that the framework was structurally limited by its origin as a legal-defensive instrument. Whether the field is willing to absorb that critique — or whether it will spend the next decade defending a measurement apparatus that has lost its political function — is the actual open question.
