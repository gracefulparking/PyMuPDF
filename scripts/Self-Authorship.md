# Scholar Stacks, Episode: Self-Authorship

[COLD OPEN]

DEV: Picture a junior at a residential liberal arts college sitting across from her advisor. She's switching majors for the third time. Last year it was pre-med because her parents are physicians. Then it was economics because her boyfriend told her she'd be good at it. Now she's saying — and this is the line that matters — "I don't actually know what I think I should do. I keep figuring out what other people want and trying to want it." That student is, in the vocabulary we're going to spend the next half hour unpacking, at the crossroads. She has noticed that the external formulas she's been following aren't hers, but she has not yet developed an internal foundation from which to choose differently. And the question that animates the entire literature we're discussing today is: how does she get from where she is to a place where she can author her own life — and is that journey the same for every student, or have we been universalizing one particular cultural pattern of becoming a self?

MAYA: That hits close. I had a version of that conversation last week with a first-gen student I'm advising — except in her case, the "external formula" wasn't her parents pushing pre-med, it was her family needing her to send money home, and what looked like "not having an internal voice yet" was actually her being deeply accountable to a web of people. And I left that meeting wondering whether the framework I'd been taught to apply to her was actually built for someone like her at all.

DEV: That's exactly the live edge of the field. So let's go back and figure out how we got here.

[pause]

[INTELLECTUAL ORIGINS]

DEV: Self-authorship, as a named construct in higher education, belongs to Marcia Baxter Magolda. She started a longitudinal study at Miami University in Ohio in 1986 — interviewing undergraduates about how they made sense of knowledge, themselves, and their relationships — and she has kept interviewing many of those same people ever since. We're talking nearly forty years of data on the same cohort aging into middle adulthood. That's the empirical spine.

MAYA: 1986. So what's the intellectual world she's stepping into?

DEV: Three things are happening simultaneously. First, William Perry's 1970 scheme — *Forms of Intellectual and Ethical Development in the College Years* — is still the dominant cognitive-developmental map in higher education. Perry interviewed Harvard undergraduates, who were all men, and traced a movement from dualism, where knowledge is right or wrong and authorities have it, through relativism, where everything is just opinion, toward commitment within relativism, where you choose a stance knowing it's contestable. Beautiful work. Built on an all-male sample at one elite institution.

MAYA: Which is the field's recurring origin sin.

DEV: It is. And the second thing happening in 1986 — same year Baxter Magolda starts her study — is the publication of Mary Belenky, Blythe Clinchy, Nancy Goldberger, and Jill Tarule's *Women's Ways of Knowing*. They're explicitly responding to Perry. They argue women's epistemological development doesn't map cleanly onto his scheme, and they name patterns — silence, received knowing, subjective knowing, procedural knowing, constructed knowing — that center voice and connection. Carol Gilligan's *In a Different Voice*, from 1982, had just given the field the methodological permission slip to say: maybe the universal developmental sequences we've inherited are actually gendered.

MAYA: So Baxter Magolda is sitting in this moment where everyone is saying Perry's not enough. Why did she need a new concept rather than just adopting Belenky or refining Perry?

DEV: Her early work, the 1992 book *Knowing and Reasoning in College*, actually is in that vein — she develops what she calls the Epistemological Reflection model and traces gender-related patterns. The pivot, the move that produces self-authorship as we now know it, happens when she encounters Robert Kegan's 1994 book *In Over Our Heads*. And this is the third stream.

Kegan is at Harvard Graduate School of Education, part of that Kohlberg-Gilligan cluster. He's a constructive-developmental theorist — meaning he believes the mind develops through structural reorganizations of how we make meaning, not just by accumulating content. His big idea is the subject-object distinction. What you are subject to, you can't see; you're embedded in it. What becomes object, you can hold up, look at, reflect on. Development is the movement of what was subject becoming object. He sequences this through orders of consciousness. The third order is the socialized mind — you're embedded in others' expectations, you can't see them as expectations because they're just how things are. The fourth order is the self-authoring mind — you can hold those expectations as object, you have an internal authority that adjudicates among them. The fifth order, self-transforming, holds even your own internal system as object.

MAYA: And Baxter Magolda essentially says, "what I've been documenting in my participants is the movement toward Kegan's fourth order"?

DEV: Yes. *Making Their Own Way*, 2001 — that's the consolidating book. Her participants are now in their late twenties and early thirties. She's watched them leave college, take jobs, get married, have crises. And she finds, in her own words, that what she had been treating too narrowly as intellectual development is actually three intertwined dimensions. She writes — and I'm quoting from the corpus here — "How we know or decide what to believe, or the epistemological dimension, is often the primary focus of college. How we view ourselves, or the intrapersonal dimension, is viewed as important but not the central focus of a college education. How we construct relationships with others, or the interpersonal dimension, is often viewed as beyond the purview of educators." And her argument is that these three move together. You can't develop an internal epistemology without an internal sense of identity, and you can't sustain either without the capacity to be in relationships without losing yourself.

MAYA: That's the integration move. Before this, the field had separate models for cognition, identity, and relationships, and they sat next to each other on the syllabus without talking.

DEV: Right. And there's an institutional condition that matters here too. The 1994 ACPA Student Learning Imperative had just reorganized student affairs around "learning" as its central concept. The field needed an integrative developmental framework that could speak the language of learning outcomes. Self-authorship, named in 2001, arrived exactly when the field had built the demand for it.

[pause]

[THE CORE FRAMEWORK]

MAYA: Okay. Let's get concrete. What does the framework actually claim?

DEV: Three dimensions, four positions. The dimensions are epistemological — how do I know? Intrapersonal — who am I? Interpersonal — how do I want to construct relationships? The positions describe a developmental movement: following external formulas, the crossroads, becoming the author of one's own life, and internal foundation.

Let me walk a student through it. Take a sophomore — call her Priya. Following external formulas: Priya knows what she believes about politics because her parents told her, she knows who she is because she's the smart kid who gets As, and her relationships are organized around being whoever the people around her need her to be. Her epistemology is "authorities have the answers." Her identity is conferred. Her relationships are about approval.

MAYA: And from the outside, she looks like she has it together.

DEV: Often she looks like the ideal student. She's compliant, she performs well, she has friends. The trouble shows up at the crossroads. Something disrupts the formulas — she fails a class, she encounters a worldview that contradicts her parents', she's in a relationship that requires her to say what she actually wants. She starts noticing that the external answers aren't working, but she doesn't yet have internal ones. Baxter Magolda quotes a participant named Gwen who describes the shift from asking "how do *you* know?" to asking "how do *I* know?" — and that question, in the corpus, is described as "the emergence of the self-authored voice as the source of knowing, an internal voice from which to interpret and judge knowledge claims from the external world."

MAYA: So the crossroads is the painful part.

DEV: It's the painful part, and Baxter Magolda finds in her longitudinal data that most of her participants are still here when they leave college. Her words: "Most of my participants made little progress toward self-authorship during college, leaving college relying on externally derived formulas for what to believe." That finding alone should make us nervous about how we talk about the four-year degree as a developmental experience.

MAYA: Wait, hold on. Most college students don't actually achieve self-authorship in college?

DEV: Correct. The developmental work largely happens in the twenties and early thirties, after the structures of college and family fall away and life starts demanding that they decide things without an external script. Which is part of why she had to extend the study — *Authoring Your Life* in 2009 follows participants into their late thirties and forties.

MAYA: What does becoming the author look like? When does Priya cross that threshold?

DEV: She starts running her beliefs through an internal filter. Not "what does my mom think, what does my professor think" — but "given what I've come to value, what do I think?" She begins to hold a sense of who she is that doesn't depend on others' confirmation. And — this is the part people skip — she becomes capable of being in close relationships without dissolving into them or controlling them. The interpersonal dimension is about, in Baxter Magolda's phrasing, the ability to engage in relationships without losing one's internal identity.

MAYA: And internal foundation?

DEV: That's when the internal voice is stable enough that it can be the place she lives from, not the place she's constantly negotiating toward. It's not the absence of doubt; it's having a philosophy of yourself robust enough to absorb doubt.

MAYA: Let me push on the pedagogy. Because Baxter Magolda doesn't just describe this — she has a model for how to teach toward it.

DEV: The Learning Partnerships Model. Three principles. Validate learners' capacity to know. Situate learning in their experience. Define learning as mutually constructing meaning. The pedagogical move is: don't tell students what to think — but also don't pretend you don't have a view. Engage them as people who are capable of working through complexity with you. The classroom version: instead of lecturing on what counts as good evidence in your field, you put a contested case in front of students, ask them how they'd weigh it, push back on their reasoning, share your reasoning, and treat the disagreement as the site of learning.

MAYA: Which sounds great if you have fifteen students in a seminar and terrible if you have three hundred in a lecture hall and an assessment regime breathing down your neck.

DEV: That tension is real, and we'll come back to it.

[pause]

[EVOLUTION AND REFINEMENTS]

MAYA: So 2001 the framework gets named. What happens to it across the next two decades?

DEV: Three big moves. First, the framework gets extended into adjacent domains. Susan Komives and her colleagues at the University of Maryland — Longerbeam, Owen, Mainella, Osteen — develop the Leadership Identity Development model, published in 2005 and 2006. Grounded theory, six stages, mapping how college students move from thinking leadership is a position the person at the top holds, toward leadership as a relational process. And in the corpus they explicitly map their stages onto Kegan: "LID stage three, leader identified, corresponded with Kegan's 1994 third order of consciousness, which he termed 'socialized.' LID stage four, leadership differentiated, corresponded with Kegan's fourth order." Self-authorship becomes the developmental skeleton of leadership education.

MAYA: So leadership pedagogy in student affairs is essentially self-authorship pedagogy in a different costume.

DEV: For a large chunk of the field, yes.

The second move is the integration with identity theory. Susan Jones and Marylu McEwen had published the Model of Multiple Dimensions of Identity in 2000 — the idea that identity is constructed across intersecting dimensions like race, gender, sexuality, class, religion, organized around a core sense of self, with different dimensions becoming salient in different contexts. They were importing intersectionality, via Kimberlé Crenshaw and Black feminist scholarship, into mainstream student affairs.

In 2007, Elisa Abes joins Jones and McEwen and they reconceptualize the model. They add what they call the "meaning-making filter." From the corpus: "Incorporating meaning-making capacity into the model provides a richer portrayal of not only *what* relationships students perceive among their personal and social identities, but also *how* they come to perceive them as they do." The idea is: a student operating with formulaic meaning-making absorbs the contextual messages about their identity unfiltered — the campus tells them what it means to be a Black woman or a queer student or a first-gen student, and they internalize that. A self-authoring student has the capacity to filter those messages through internal commitments.

MAYA: Which sounds like a genuine integration. But you and I both know there's a critique embedded in that move.

DEV: Right. Read sympathetically, it's integration — identity theory and developmental theory finally talking. Read critically, the developmental framework absorbed the identity framework. Self-authorship became the *filter*, the more developed capacity. The implicit ordering is that meaning-making that runs through an individual internal voice is more mature than meaning-making that runs through community, family, ancestry. The Abes-Jones-McEwen piece itself flags this concern — they note in the corpus that "student development theory in general has been slow to move in a direction that considers relationships between power structures and the fluidity of development."

The third move is empirical. The Wabash National Study of Liberal Arts Education, launched in 2006 out of Wabash College, used self-authorship as a key construct in large-N quantitative work. So a framework that had been built on a qualitative longitudinal study of fewer than a hundred people suddenly had statistical instruments and tens of thousands of student responses behind it.

MAYA: So is the original Baxter Magolda still usable, or has all the action moved to the extensions and revisions?

DEV: She herself is still working — she's collaborated with Jane Pizzolato, with Rosemary Perez, with critical scholars trying to revise the framework in response to cultural critique. Her current position, as I understand it, is that the developmental movement is universal but the *content* — what an internal foundation looks like, what relationships look like at the foundational position — is culturally variable. Critics say that concedes too little. We'll get there.

[pause]

[LIVE DEBATES]

MAYA: Let's name the live debates. Where is this contested?

DEV: At least two substantive lines of critique. The first is the cultural-universalism critique. The argument, coming most pointedly from Indigenous scholars and from critical race scholars working with Tara Yosso's community cultural wealth framework, is that self-authorship valorizes a particular Western, individualist, modernist conception of the self. The developmental endpoint — an internally-defined foundation from which one authors one's own life — sounds neutral, but it isn't. For students from communities where knowledge is relational, where authority is ancestral or communal, where identity is constituted through obligation to kin and place, the framework can pathologize their meaning-making as developmentally behind.

MAYA: Yosso's argument is that things the dominant frame reads as deficits — relying on family, navigating through community networks, drawing on resistance knowledge — are actually forms of capital, just not the kind universities recognize.

DEV: Right. And Bryan Brayboy's TribalCrit work pushes this further. In many Indigenous epistemologies, the very idea of a sovereign internal author is incoherent — knowledge is located in relation, in land, in ancestors, in story. To say a student needs to develop an internal foundation separate from external authorities is to ask them to undo a worldview, not develop into one.

MAYA: What would Baxter Magolda say back?

DEV: I think her sincere response, and the response of people like Patricia King, is that they distinguish the developmental capacity from its cultural expression. The capacity to reflect on, hold, and adjudicate among the voices in your life is the universal piece. Whether the resulting foundation is articulated as individual choice or as accountability to ancestors is the cultural piece. They'd say a self-authoring Indigenous student can be deeply committed to communal and ancestral authority — but committed in a way that's been reflectively chosen rather than unreflectively inherited.

MAYA: That's slippery though. Because if every form of meaning-making can be reframed as either reflective or unreflective version of itself, the framework becomes unfalsifiable. And it still privileges the reflective version.

DEV: That's the critics' counter. The move to "we just mean reflective commitment" preserves the developmental hierarchy while neutralizing the critique. And it assumes that reflection-on is always developmentally superior to embeddedness-in — which is itself a culturally specific claim.

MAYA: Where do you land?

DEV: I think the framework describes something real that is happening in some students' lives — the experience of noticing that you've been running other people's scripts and beginning to write your own. I don't think it describes the only or the most mature form of human development. And I think when we deploy it as a learning outcome at the institutional level, we are quietly imposing one cultural model of personhood as the goal of higher education. Which we should be more honest about.

MAYA: I want to push you on something. You said the framework describes something real. But I'm not sure that's separable from the cultural critique. Because the experience of "noticing you've been running other people's scripts" is itself an experience that's more salient in cultures that teach you the scripts are *yours* to revise. In a context where your scripts are understood as your inheritance — your debt, your gift — the "noticing" might not feel like development. It might feel like betrayal.

DEV: That's fair. And it's exactly the move that some of the more recent critical work makes — that the framework's basic phenomenology isn't culturally neutral.

MAYA: What's the second critique?

DEV: This one comes from a different direction — queer theory and post-structuralism, most clearly in Elisa Abes's 2009 piece on theoretical borderlands and Z Nicolazzo's work on trans students. The charge isn't that self-authorship privileges Western culture — it's that the very ideal of a stable, integrated internal foundation reproduces a modernist sovereign self that queer and trans experience contests. The self that the framework moves you toward is coherent, integrated, agentic, continuous. But what if the self is plural, fluid, contextual, performatively constituted? What if the developmental task isn't integration but the capacity to hold incoherence?

MAYA: And Kegan's own fifth order — the self-transforming mind — actually hints at this. That the fourth-order achievement is itself something to be moved through.

DEV: Right. The fifth order rarely shows up in the student affairs literature because the fourth order is hard enough as a pedagogical target. But the queer-theoretic critique is essentially saying: you've made a developmental ideal out of a transitional achievement, and you're missing both what comes before and what comes after.

[pause]

[WHAT THE FIELD GETS WRONG]

DEV: Here's the thing practitioners flatten. Self-authorship gets translated, in division-level learning outcomes documents and in advising conversations, as "helping students develop autonomy" or "fostering critical thinking" or "supporting independent decision-making." And those translations are wrong in a specific way.

Self-authorship is not autonomy in the colloquial sense — it's not pulling away from others, not figuring things out alone, not rejecting external influence. The interpersonal dimension is right there in the framework. The capacity to be in relationships without losing oneself is half the point. A student who has rejected their family and decided what they think by themselves has not achieved self-authorship — they may have simply traded one external formula for an oppositional one.

MAYA: And critical thinking isn't the same thing either.

DEV: Not at all. A student can think critically about a text while still operating epistemologically as a follower of external formulas — they're just deploying the critical method the professor taught them. Self-authorship is about where the *source* of judgment lives, not about the technical quality of the reasoning. You can be a brilliant analyst and a developmental third-order thinker.

MAYA: I'd add — and this connects to your point about the corpus finding — practitioners flatten the timeline. They talk about self-authorship as a four-year outcome of college. Baxter Magolda's own data says most students leave college without it. The honest version is that college can move some students from external formulas to the crossroads, and maybe set the conditions for the rest to happen in their twenties. If we put "students will demonstrate self-authorship by graduation" on a learning outcomes document, we are promising something the founding research says we usually can't deliver.

DEV: That's exactly right. The framework gets institutionalized in a form the framework itself contradicts.

[pause]

[IMPLICATIONS FOR YOUR WORK]

MAYA: Okay, let me take this where I think it lands for our audience. If you're doing dissertation work that touches on student development, identity, leadership, or transitions, what does this episode change about your design?

First, if you're using self-authorship as your theoretical framework, you need to name the cultural-universalism critique in your literature review, not bury it in a limitations section at the end. The Abes 2009 piece, the Yosso work, the more recent Perez extensions — these are not optional citations anymore. Your committee should be asking you why your participants' meaning-making should be read through this lens specifically.

Second, if your sample includes students from communities where collective, ancestral, or relational authority is central — Indigenous students, students from immigrant families, students from tightly held religious communities — you need to be ready to find what looks like "reliance on external authority" and not auto-code it as developmentally early. That coding decision is the place where the framework's bias enters the analysis.

DEV: I'd add a methodological point. The vast majority of self-authorship research is qualitative interview work, with the Wabash Study as the main quantitative exception. If you're a doctoral student looking for a contribution, the underdeveloped methodologies — ethnographic, participatory, narrative-historical, longitudinal in non-residential contexts — are wide open. We don't know what self-authorship looks like in community college populations, in working-class students whose adult lives don't conform to the white-collar trajectory the longitudinal sample mostly took, in students whose religious communities make "moving beyond external formulas" theologically incoherent.

MAYA: For practitioners — Monday morning version. If you're advising a student at what looks like the crossroads, the Learning Partnerships Model is genuinely useful. Don't rescue them with an answer. Validate their capacity to work this out. Tell them what you actually think when they ask, but frame it as your reasoning, not as the answer. Make the disagreement productive rather than resolving it.

DEV: And — this is the harder version — notice when the student you're advising isn't actually at the crossroads, they're at home in a way of knowing that doesn't fit your framework. Don't try to push them through a developmental transition they may not need or want.

MAYA: For administrators writing learning outcomes — please stop putting "self-authorship" on the four-year deliverables. Put "movement toward self-authorship" or "capacity for self-authored reasoning in domain X." The founding research doesn't support the stronger claim and you're setting your assessment people up to measure something that mostly happens after graduation.

DEV: For faculty — the Learning Partnerships Model is not a curriculum overhaul. It's a stance. You can adopt it in a single seminar discussion. The question is whether you're willing to put your own reasoning on the table as reasoning rather than as authority, and whether you're willing to treat student disagreement as evidence of developmental work rather than as resistance to be managed.

[pause]

[READING ROADMAP]

DEV: Four texts, in order.

Start with Kegan's *In Over Our Heads*, 1994. You need to understand the constructive-developmental architecture — subject-object, the orders of consciousness — before Baxter Magolda's vocabulary will make sense. Otherwise self-authorship sounds like a slogan rather than a structural claim about meaning-making. The Harkins review in the corpus is a useful orientation if you want to know why the field received this book the way it did.

Second, Baxter Magolda's *Making Their Own Way*, 2001. This is the consolidating statement. The three dimensions, the four positions, the longitudinal warrant. Read it with attention to who her participants actually are — the demographics are in the methodology section and they will frame everything else.

Third, Abes, Jones, and McEwen, 2007, "Reconceptualizing the Model of Multiple Dimensions of Identity" in the *Journal of College Student Development*. This is where self-authorship gets installed into identity theory as the meaning-making filter. Read it both as an integration and as a takeover — both readings are defensible and you should be able to argue either.

Fourth, jump to Susan Jones and Elisa Abes's *Identity Development of College Students*, 2013. This is the book that holds self-authorship in productive tension with critical and post-structural frameworks. It's where you'll see what the field looks like when it takes its own critiques seriously rather than running parallel citation tracks.

If you want a fifth, the Torres, Jones, and Renn 2009 piece in the *Journal of College Student Development*'s 50th anniversary issue is the field's own self-portrait at the moment the critical turn was becoming undeniable.

[pause]

[WHAT WE DON'T KNOW]

DEV: One honest gap. We don't know what self-authorship looks like — or whether it's even the right developmental construct — for students whose lives are organized by economic precarity. The framework was built on participants who, however diverse in some respects, had the time and the material stability to do the slow, often painful work of revising their epistemology, their sense of self, and their relationships across their twenties and thirties. The developmental task of authoring an internal foundation may be a task you can take up only if your hours of the day and your weight of obligation permit it. For a student working two jobs to keep their family housed, the question "how do I know what I believe?" may simply not be available in the way it was for Baxter Magolda's longitudinal participants. And we don't have a developmental theory that takes that seriously — not as a limitation of access, but as a fundamental reorganization of what development is and who gets to do it.
