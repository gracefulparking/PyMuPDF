# Scholar Stacks — Student Involvement and Engagement

[COLD OPEN]

DEV: Here's a sentence from a 1984 article that ended up restructuring an entire field. Alexander Astin writes that what determines whether college changes a student is "the amount of physical and psychological energy that the student devotes to the academic experience." Not the curriculum. Not the institution's selectivity. Not the faculty's pedigree. The student's energy. And then he gives an example so mundane it's almost embarrassing — students who live in residence halls do better, and they do better because, in his words, they are "eating, sleeping, and spending their waking hours" inside a campus environment. That's the mechanism.

MAYA: It sounds almost like a parent's intuition dressed up as theory. Show up, put the hours in, you'll get something out.

DEV: Right. And yet that sentence — that argument — is the load-bearing wall under NSSE, under high-impact practices, under most of what student affairs spent the next forty years saying about itself. So the puzzle I want to sit with today is: how did such a simple causal claim get so much institutional weight? And what did the field have to give up — and what did it have to ignore — to make it carry that weight?

MAYA: This is live for me because I'm working on a study of first-generation commuter students at a regional public, and every framework I pick up assumes my students are doing something they mostly can't do. They have jobs. They have kids. They drive forty minutes each way. "Engagement," operationally, looks like a luxury good in my data. So I want to know whether the concept can be salvaged, whether it should be, or whether I'm using the wrong lens entirely.

[pause]

[INTELLECTUAL ORIGINS]

DEV: Let's locate Astin. He's at UCLA by 1973, running the Cooperative Institutional Research Program — CIRP — which he'd founded in 1966 at the American Council on Education. CIRP gave him something almost no one else in higher education had: longitudinal data on hundreds of thousands of students across hundreds of institutions. The Freshman Survey on the front end, follow-ups on the back end. That data infrastructure is what made the input-environment-output model — I-E-O — possible in the first place.

MAYA: And I-E-O is the architecture under involvement, right? Inputs are who the students are when they arrive, environment is what the institution does to them, outputs are what they look like at the end.

DEV: Exactly, and that's a Lewinian architecture. Kurt Lewin's field theory — behavior is a function of person and environment — gets imported into educational psychology in the postwar period, and Astin builds his model on that joint dependence. What's distinctive about the 1984 paper is that Astin had been running I-E-O regressions for almost two decades and kept finding that environmental variables — institutional characteristics, resources, curriculum — explained much less of the variance in outcomes than people expected. So he's responding to his own data. The prestige-and-resources story wasn't working empirically.

MAYA: Wait — why did the field need a new concept, though? There were already retention theories. Tinto's "Dropout from Higher Education" came out in 1975.

DEV: Good question, and Tinto is the ghost in this room the whole episode. Vincent Tinto imports Durkheim — anomie, social integration — into student departure research. His framework says students leave when they fail to integrate academically and socially into the institution. Astin and Tinto are looking at overlapping phenomena, but their theoretical centers are different. Tinto's mechanism is integration: the student matches the institution or doesn't. Astin's mechanism is energy: the student invests or doesn't. Tinto puts the action at the boundary between person and institution. Astin puts it inside the student.

MAYA: And that's a consequential difference for practice. If you're Tinto, you redesign the institution to be more integratable. If you're Astin, you design experiences that pull energy out of the student.

DEV: And it matters politically too. Remember what was happening in 1984. Reagan had proposed deep cuts to student aid in 1981. *A Nation at Risk* dropped in April 1983 — K-12, but the rhetorical template of measurable outcomes was now national. In July 1984, two things happen almost simultaneously: Astin's article appears in the *Journal of College Student Personnel*, and the National Institute of Education releases *Involvement in Learning*, chaired by Kenneth Mortimer, with Astin as a study group member. The NIE report nationalizes Astin's vocabulary. Within eighteen months you also get the AAC's *Integrity in the College Curriculum*. That's a discursive event, not just a publication.

MAYA: So involvement gets a federal stamp at exactly the moment the federal government is asking what it's getting for its money.

DEV: Right. And student affairs was hungry for this. The field had been operating off the 1937 and 1949 *Student Personnel Point of View* statements — counseling psychology, in-loco-parentis residue — and by the late seventies it was clear that wasn't going to survive as a professional identity. Chickering's *Education and Identity* in 1969 had given them psychosocial development. Perry's 1970 book had given them cognitive development. Astin gave them an empirical mechanism that put student affairs activities — residence life, student organizations, on-campus employment — squarely inside the production function of college outcomes.

MAYA: Which is why Astin's own corpus evidence is so telling. He writes that the residential effect held "in all types of institutions and among all types of students regardless of sex, race, ability, or family background." That's an enormous universal claim.

DEV: It's the kind of claim you can only make from CIRP-scale data, and it's also the kind of claim that turns out to be the concept's biggest vulnerability later. Hold onto it.

[pause]

[THE CORE FRAMEWORK]

DEV: Let's get the postulates on the table, because the 1984 paper is unusually disciplined for student affairs theory — Astin gives you five testable claims.

One: involvement refers to the investment of physical and psychological energy in objects — a task, a person, an activity. Two: regardless of the object, involvement occurs along a continuum; different students show different degrees, and the same student shows different degrees in different things at different times. Three — and this is the one people forget — involvement has both quantitative and qualitative features. How much time you spend studying, and how focused you are while doing it. Four: the amount of student learning and development associated with any educational program is directly proportional to the quality and quantity of student involvement. Five: the effectiveness of any educational policy or practice is related to its capacity to increase involvement.

MAYA: So the fifth postulate is a design principle for institutions. Astin is saying: judge your policies by whether they pull more energy out of students.

DEV: Yes, and that's the move that travels. Let me make this concrete. Imagine a sophomore — call her Daniela — at a four-year public. She lives off-campus with her aunt, works twenty-five hours a week at a pharmacy, takes fifteen credits, doesn't join clubs. By Astin's framework, the diagnostic question isn't "is Daniela a good student?" It's "where is her energy going, and how much of it reaches the academic experience?"

MAYA: And here's where I get nervous. Because Astin would look at Daniela and say: low involvement, low predicted outcomes. But the twenty-five hours at the pharmacy might be where most of her developmental work is happening — supporting family, managing money, navigating adult systems.

DEV: That's the right critique and we'll get there. But stay inside the framework for a second, because Astin actually anticipates part of this. He distinguishes — and this is the corpus quote — between "the content and resource approaches to pedagogy" that "tend to favor the well-prepared, assertive student" and an involvement approach that asks instructors to think about every student's energy allocation. He's explicitly worried about the passive student. His policy lever is: redesign the academic experience to demand and reward involvement from students who wouldn't volunteer it.

MAYA: So in Daniela's case, an Astinian intervention would be — what — a required first-year seminar that meets at a time she can attend? Embedded peer mentoring? Faculty office hours scheduled around work?

DEV: Those are exactly the moves. And notice what happened by the late nineties. George Kuh takes Astin's mechanism and operationalizes it. NSSE launches in 1999, with funding from Russell Edgerton at Pew, and the instrument measures behaviors — how often did you discuss ideas with faculty outside class, how often did you work with classmates on projects, how many pages did you write. It's Astin's "quantity and quality of energy invested" turned into a survey item.

MAYA: And then by 2008 Kuh names the high-impact practices — first-year seminars, learning communities, undergraduate research, internships, capstones. So the lineage runs: Astin's postulates, NSSE's measurement, HIPs as the prescriptive list.

DEV: Right. And here's the move that's worth pausing on. Astin's theory was a regression specification — it told you what variables to put on the right-hand side. Kuh's NSSE is an accountability instrument. Those are different objects. A theory describes a mechanism; an accountability instrument shapes institutional behavior. The translation from one to the other is itself a major event in how higher education research influenced institutional practice — and it's also where the concept starts taking on weight it wasn't engineered for.

MAYA: Can I push on the universal claim again? Astin says the residential effect holds across sex, race, ability, family background. But by the early eighties, women had been a majority of undergraduates for several years, Black enrollment had doubled since 1965, part-time and adult students were the fastest-growing category. Was that universal claim even plausible at the time?

DEV: It was plausible in the data Astin had, which was CIRP — heavily four-year, heavily traditional-age. The universal claim is partly an artifact of who was in the sample. And that's not me being clever in hindsight; Pace and Stern had been measuring college environments since the late fifties, Pace's College Student Experiences Questionnaire in 1979 was already capturing variation Astin's I-E-O model was smoothing over. The universal language was a choice, not a forced inference.

[pause]

[EVOLUTION AND REFINEMENTS]

DEV: So what happens to the concept after 1984? Two parallel things. One is empirical consolidation — Pascarella and Terenzini publish *How College Affects Students* in 1991, nine hundred pages, and Astin's framework gets canonical status alongside Tinto's. The 1996 ACPA *Student Learning Imperative* and the 1998 ACPA-NASPA *Powerful Partnerships* explicitly ground the field in Astin, Pascarella, and Chickering. Involvement is now the official theoretical scaffolding of student affairs.

MAYA: And the second thing?

DEV: The second thing is critique, from several directions, and the critiques mostly do not displace Astin — they layer on top of him. Let's take them in order.

Mary Belenky and her co-authors publish *Women's Ways of Knowing* in 1986, arguing that the dominant developmental frameworks were built on male samples and missed how women constructed knowledge. Marcia Baxter Magolda's *Knowing and Reasoning in College* in 1992 extends that. Carolyn Cross's *Shades of Black* in 1991 challenges universalist developmental claims around race. None of these are direct attacks on Astin, but they collectively erode the universality his framework rested on.

MAYA: And then Laura Rendón.

DEV: Laura Rendón's "Validation Theory" in 1994 is more pointed. Rendón says — and this is the live disagreement with Astin — that involvement theory assumes students arrive ready to engage. For first-generation students, students of color, returning adults, the prior experience may have actively taught them they don't belong. They need validation from institutional agents before involvement is even possible. So Rendón doesn't reject involvement; she says there's a step zero Astin didn't theorize.

MAYA: William Tierney does something stronger, right?

DEV: Tierney's 1992 article in the *Journal of Higher Education* — "An Anthropological Analysis of Student Participation in College" — targets Tinto explicitly but the argument applies to Astin. He says the integration model treats minoritized students as needing to assimilate into a dominant campus culture, and that this is an assimilationist demand dressed up as developmental science. That's a structural critique.

MAYA: So is the original involvement theory still usable, or has it been so heavily qualified that we should retire it?

DEV: I'd say it's still usable as a research-question generator, but it's not usable as a complete theory of student experience. Two things have happened. First, the construct has been disaggregated — Susan Jones and Marylu McEwen's 1996 work on the Multiple Dimensions of Identity, then Abes, Jones and McEwen's 2007 reconceptualization that inserts a meaning-making filter, have made identity central in a way Astin's framework couldn't accommodate. Second, the locus of analysis has shifted. Look at what Sam Museus and Uma Jayakumar do in *Creating Campus Cultures* in 2012, and then Museus's CECE model in 2014.

MAYA: Quote the CECE piece for me.

DEV: Museus writes that "traditional student success frameworks, such as Tinto's integration theory, and the research that has employed them to study student success have focused more on students' behaviors than the environmental and psychological elements of college success." That's the structural reframing. The CECE model proposes nine indicators — collectivist cultural orientations, humanized educational environments, proactive philosophies, holistic support, and so on — that locate engagement in what the campus does, not what the student does.

MAYA: But Museus is still building an instrument with indicators, which is the Kuh-NSSE logic.

DEV: Right, and that's the most interesting thing about CECE. Museus inherits the measurement infrastructure even as he inverts the locus of responsibility. He's playing on Astin's field, with Astin's rules, against Astin's assumptions. That's why CECE has institutional traction Yosso's pure CRT critique didn't have on its own.

MAYA: Speaking of Yosso — Tara Yosso's 2005 piece "Whose Culture Has Capital?" is doing something different than CECE, isn't it?

DEV: Yosso inverts Bourdieu. Bourdieu's cultural capital framework, when applied to higher education, tends to read working-class and minoritized students as capital-deficient. Yosso names six forms of community cultural wealth — aspirational, navigational, social, linguistic, familial, resistant — that communities of color possess and that institutions fail to recognize. Applied to the engagement framework, Yosso's argument is that what gets measured as "involvement" is a particular cultural form of participation, and that students bringing other forms of engagement — family obligations, community organizing, religious community — are being read as underengaged against an unmarked white middle-class baseline.

[pause]

[LIVE DEBATES]

MAYA: Where is this concept actively contested right now?

DEV: Let me name three live debates. The first is the descriptive-versus-normative question. Astin and Kuh treat involvement and engagement as empirical mechanisms — variables you can measure that predict outcomes. Critics treat engagement as a normative ideal that smuggles in assumptions about what a real student looks like. Anthony Jack's *The Privileged Poor* in 2019 makes this concrete: he shows that even among low-income students admitted to elite colleges, "involvement" is differentially accessible depending on whether they attended elite high schools. The script for office hours, study groups, faculty mentorship — it's class-coded. So "engagement" measured at the surface hides massive variation in capacity to engage.

MAYA: And the defender's response?

DEV: The defender — call this the Kuh position — would say: that's exactly why we should be measuring engagement, because it makes the gap visible. If engagement is class-coded, NSSE will detect that, and institutions can intervene. The instrument is a tool of equity, not a normative imposition.

MAYA: That sounds defensible, but I want to push on it. NSSE didn't just measure engagement — it shaped what institutions did to score better on engagement. So when Sara Goldrick-Rab publishes *Paying the Price* in 2016 and documents widespread basic needs insecurity, she's pointing at something that NSSE was structurally not designed to see. You can't engage if you're food insecure. The instrument's blind spots became institutional blind spots.

DEV: I think that's right, and it leads into the second debate, which is whose responsibility engagement is. Astin's postulates put the energy on the student. CECE puts the locus on the campus culture. Most institutions operate as if both are true and design interventions accordingly. That's convenient politically but theoretically incoherent. You can't simultaneously hold students responsible for their engagement and hold institutions responsible for the conditions that make engagement possible without specifying which dominates when they conflict.

MAYA: Here's where I want to push back on you, Dev. You've been pretty even-handed, but I think you're underselling the structural critique. Estela Bensimon's equity-minded practice work at USC's Center for Urban Education argues that "engagement gaps" are produced by institutions and then attributed to students. That's not just a layered-on critique. That's an argument that the whole construct, as operationalized, gets the causal direction backwards.

DEV: Fair. I'll go further. The Bensimon argument is that disaggregating outcomes by race and asking "why are these students underperforming" already assumes the institution is fine and the students are the variable. Equity-minded practice flips it: why is this institution underperforming for these students? That's not a refinement of engagement theory — it's a reversal of where you point the diagnostic instrument.

MAYA: And the third debate?

DEV: The third is the post-pandemic one, which I think the field hasn't reckoned with yet. Astin's mechanism assumed physical presence — eating, sleeping, waking hours on campus. NSSE assumed it. The high-impact practices framework assumed it. COVID collapsed that assumption in March 2020. We now have permanent online and hybrid modalities, and we have generative AI raising questions about what "psychological energy devoted to academic experience" even means when cognitive work can be partially outsourced. Add to that the belonging research from Gregory Walton and Geoffrey Cohen at Stanford, which has partly displaced engagement as the dominant lens on student experience — measuring something more affective and more amenable to brief interventions.

MAYA: Is belonging just engagement with better packaging?

DEV: I don't think so. Belonging is a felt sense; engagement is a behavioral pattern. You can be highly engaged and not feel like you belong, and that combination is associated with worse outcomes than the reverse. So they're related but not nested. The belonging literature also has tighter experimental backing — Walton's brief social-belonging interventions have been replicated, with caveats, in ways NSSE-style engagement claims usually aren't.

MAYA: Where do you actually land on whether involvement is still a viable theoretical foundation?

DEV: I land on this: Astin's mechanism is real but narrow. Energy invested predicts outcomes, with a lot of qualifications about what counts as energy and what counts as the relevant outcome. The framework is most useful when you treat it as a starting hypothesis to be specified by context, not as a universal model. Where I get critical is the institutionalization — NSSE, HIPs, the whole apparatus — which took a partial mechanism and built a national accountability regime on it. That's a category error, even if the underlying observation is sound.

[pause]

[WHAT THE FIELD GETS WRONG]

DEV: Here's the thing practitioners routinely flatten. When people invoke "engagement," they almost always mean activity. Did students attend events, join clubs, show up for programming. Astin's third postulate is explicit: involvement has both quantitative and qualitative features. The qualitative dimension — what he calls the depth and focus of the engagement — almost always drops out in practice because it's harder to measure.

MAYA: Give me the concrete version of that.

DEV: A student who attends every event, joins four clubs, holds two officer positions — by the activity count, that's a maximally engaged student. By Astin's actual theory, you have no idea, because you haven't measured the quality of psychological investment. The student could be performing involvement as resume-building, with shallow attention. Another student who's deeply absorbed in two activities and indifferent to everything else might be more involved in Astin's terms. The accountability infrastructure rewards the first student and is structurally blind to the second.

MAYA: And the consequence?

DEV: The consequence is that institutions over-program. They generate more activities to be engaged with, because activity counts are what they're measured on, when the underlying theory said the quality of the engagement is at least as important. We've optimized for the measurable half of a two-part construct.

The second flattening is the deficit reading. When practitioners say a student is "disengaged," they almost always mean the student isn't doing what the institution offers. Astin's framework can support that reading, but it doesn't require it. The same student could be heavily engaged in family, work, off-campus community — energy is being invested somewhere. The deficit reading happens when "engagement" gets implicitly defined as "engagement in institutionally-sponsored activities." That's the move Yosso and Museus are objecting to.

[pause]

[IMPLICATIONS FOR YOUR WORK]

MAYA: Let me try to bring this down to specific implications.

If you're a doctoral student studying student experience — like I am with first-generation commuters — this framework suggests you should not use engagement as a dependent variable measured by activity counts. You should treat it as a contested construct that needs to be specified in your particular context. Ask what counts as energy investment for your population. For my students, that might be sustained attention during the limited campus time they have, or the quality of one or two key relationships with faculty, not breadth of activity.

DEV: I'd add: if you're using NSSE data or institutional engagement data, you should explicitly theorize what the instrument is and isn't capturing. Don't treat NSSE scores as engagement; treat them as one operationalization of engagement, with known blind spots — basic needs, commuter populations, online students, cultural variation in participation norms.

MAYA: If you're a student affairs practitioner — say, a residence life director or a first-year experience coordinator — Monday morning, what changes?

DEV: I think two things. One: stop using participation rates as your primary metric for whether your programming is working. Ask qualitative questions about the depth of what's happening. Two: take seriously that some of your students' developmental work is happening off your campus, in their workplaces, in their families. The institutional question isn't "how do I pull more of their energy onto campus" — that's the Astin reading, and it can be paternalistic. The institutional question is "what energy investments matter for their development, and which of those can I support?"

MAYA: For faculty?

DEV: For faculty — and this is straight from Astin, actually — design assignments and class structures that require engagement rather than assuming it. The 1984 paper is pretty pointed about this. Astin writes that content and resource approaches to pedagogy "favor the well-prepared, assertive student." That's a forty-year-old indictment of the lecture-and-office-hours model that's still operative. Required structured peer interaction, scaffolded research projects, embedded reflection — these are the moves the theory implies.

MAYA: For administrators and people doing institutional accountability work?

DEV: This is where I'd be most pointed. NSSE was designed as a corrective to U.S. News rankings — to shift accountability from inputs to practices. That mission was real, and partly succeeded. But twenty-five years in, NSSE has become the thing it was meant to disrupt: a benchmarking instrument that institutions optimize against. If you're an administrator, the question is whether you're using engagement measurement to learn about your institution or to perform accountability to external audiences. Those produce different kinds of action.

MAYA: And for researchers doing equity work specifically?

DEV: Read Museus alongside Astin, not as a replacement for him. The CECE model is doing something theoretically sophisticated — it accepts the measurement-infrastructure logic and inverts the locus of responsibility. If you're studying engagement gaps, the CECE indicators give you a vocabulary for asking what the institution is doing that produces those gaps. That's a more usable framework for equity-oriented research than asking the same questions Astin asked with a more diverse sample.

[pause]

[READING ROADMAP]

DEV: Four texts, in order, for a doctoral student working into this concept.

Start with Astin 1984 itself — "Student Involvement: A Developmental Theory for Higher Education" in what was then the *Journal of College Student Personnel*. Read it for the postulates and for the empirical confidence. You need to know what the original argument actually said before you read forty years of commentary on it. It's also, frankly, a short paper. There's no excuse not to read it.

Second, George Kuh's 2008 *High-Impact Educational Practices*, published by AAC&U. This is the institutionalization document. Read it to understand how Astin's mechanism got translated into a prescriptive list, and read it critically — ask which of the ten practices have strong evidence and which are mostly inherited assumption.

Third, Tara Yosso's 2005 "Whose Culture Has Capital?" in *Race Ethnicity and Education*. This is where the deficit assumption built into the engagement tradition gets named and inverted. Read it after Kuh, not before, because you need to see the institutional framework Yosso is critiquing.

Fourth, Sam Museus's articulation of the CECE model — the 2014 paper or the Museus, Yi, and Saelua 2017 piece in *The Review of Higher Education*. Read it last because it's doing the reconstructive work — accepting Astin's measurement logic and inverting his assumptions about where responsibility for engagement lies. The Museus piece only makes its full sense after you've seen what it's trying to repair.

If you have time for a fifth, Patricia King and Marcia Baxter Magolda's 1996 piece on developmental learning, then Baxter Magolda's *Making Their Own Way* in 2001, give you the cognitive-developmental tradition that runs parallel to the engagement strand and that informs the meaning-making filter Abes inserted into the MMDI in 2007. That parallel tradition matters because it's where the field theorizes the qualitative side of involvement — the depth dimension Astin named but didn't develop.

[pause]

[WHAT WE DON'T KNOW]

MAYA: Where's the honest gap?

DEV: Here's the one I keep coming back to. The engagement literature was built on a residential, full-time, traditional-age student model, and the field has known for thirty years that this isn't who most undergraduates are. We've layered critiques on top of the framework, we've added cultural and equity dimensions, we've revised NSSE multiple times. But we don't actually have a theory of involvement for the part-time, working, commuting, online, adult student that is empirically grounded in their experience rather than retrofitted from the residential model. Anthony Jack got at part of this for low-income students at elite colleges. Goldrick-Rab got at part of it through basic needs. But the construct itself — what does psychological energy invested in academic experience mean for a thirty-four-year-old taking two online courses while working full-time and raising two children? — that's not theorized. We've been measuring those students against a model that wasn't built for them, calling the gap "underengagement," and then designing interventions to pull them into a campus shape that doesn't fit their lives. The field's been honest about the demographic shift since the early nineties. It hasn't been honest enough about the fact that we still don't have the theory we'd need to take that shift seriously.
