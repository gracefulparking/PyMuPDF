# Scholar Stacks Episode: Student Involvement and Engagement

[COLD OPEN]

DEV: In 1984, Alexander Astin published a fourteen-page article in the *Journal of College Student Personnel* that basically said: stop arguing about which colleges are best. You're measuring the wrong thing. What matters is not where students go — it's what they do once they get there. The amount of physical and psychological energy a student devotes to the academic experience. That's the active ingredient. Everything else — selectivity, endowment size, faculty credentials — is, at best, a proxy.

MAYA: Which sounds almost too obvious now, right? Like, of course what students do matters.

DEV: Right, but think about what that argument was doing in 1984. *U.S. News* had just started ranking colleges the year before. Reagan's administration was trying to dismantle the Department of Education. *A Nation at Risk* had landed the year before that, calling American education a national security threat. Into all of that, Astin essentially says: the prestige economy you're building, the rankings, the inputs obsession — none of it is the thing.

MAYA: And this matters for my work right now because I'm sitting in a dissertation prospectus committee meeting last week, and someone asks me, "How are you operationalizing engagement?" And I have a NSSE-shaped answer in my head, which is fine. But I can't actually tell them where the construct came from, what it's load-bearing for, or what it might be hiding. And it turns out that gap — between "engagement" as a measurement tool and "involvement" as a theoretical claim — is the whole story.

DEV: That gap is the episode.

[pause]

[INTELLECTUAL ORIGINS]

DEV: So let's locate Astin properly. The 1984 article is treated as the origin point, but it's really a consolidation. Astin had been building the input-environment-output model — the I-E-O model — since the late 1960s. He founded the Cooperative Institutional Research Program, CIRP, at ACE in 1966, moved it to UCLA in '73, and by 1977 he'd published *Four Critical Years*, which is the empirical book. The 1984 piece is the theory paper that catches up to twenty years of data.

MAYA: Wait — why did he need a new concept in '84 if the data had been there since the seventies?

DEV: Two reasons. One is intellectual: the data kept showing that institutional characteristics — selectivity, expenditures, faculty-student ratios — explained much less variance in student outcomes than anyone in the field wanted to admit. The student-level behavioral variables kept eating the institutional variables. He needed a theory to explain why.

The second reason is political. 1984 is also the year the National Institute of Education publishes *Involvement in Learning*, the Mortimer report. Astin was on that study group. The National Governors Association puts out *Time for Results* in '86. States start passing assessment mandates. There's suddenly a policy demand for an answer to "what should we hold colleges accountable for?" And Astin has a framework ready that says: hold them accountable for what students do.

MAYA: So the concept emerges at the exact moment institutions need a defensible answer to an accountability question.

DEV: Right. And here's the actual claim, in Astin's own words from the 1984 piece. He defines involvement using a string of verbs — "commit oneself, devote oneself, engage in, participate in, plunge into, show enthusiasm for, take part in, take to, take up, undertake." That's the conceptual texture. Then he gives five postulates. The two that matter most: involvement occurs along a continuum — different students manifest different degrees in different objects at different times — and involvement has both quantitative and qualitative features.

MAYA: So it's not just hours logged.

DEV: It's not just hours logged. Though Astin's empirical examples lean heavily quantitative. The classic finding he reports — and this is direct quote — "Living in a campus residence was positively related to retention, and this positive effect occurred in all types of institutions and among all types of students regardless of sex, race, ability, or family background." That's the kind of finding the theory is built to explain. Residential students get more involved because — and again, his language — "simply by eating, sleeping, and spending their waking hours on campus" they're exposed to more involvement opportunities.

MAYA: Which is a sentence I'd want to push on.

DEV: We'll push on it. But hold the lineage for a second. Astin is working in a tradition that has at least three streams feeding it. There's behaviorist learning theory — the time-on-task literature from Bloom, Fisher, Gagné that Astin cites explicitly. He says, quote, "time-on-task and effort appear frequently in the literature as key determinants of a wide range of cognitive learning outcomes." That's the educational psychology base.

The second stream is Vincent Tinto's interactionalist model of student departure, 1975, revised in *Leaving College* in '87. Tinto's framework is sociological — Durkheim's suicide study reworked for college dropout, with academic and social integration as the key constructs. Astin and Tinto end up running on parallel tracks for thirty years, with people constantly conflating "involvement" and "integration." They're not the same thing. Astin is more psychological — energy investment by an individual. Tinto is more sociological — fit between student and institutional subsystems.

The third stream is C. Robert Pace's "quality of effort" work at UCLA's Higher Education Research Institute. Pace developed the College Student Experiences Questionnaire in 1979. That's actually the direct methodological ancestor of what becomes NSSE. George Kuh credits Pace explicitly when he builds the National Survey of Student Engagement in 1998.

MAYA: So the line is Pace's CSEQ to Kuh's NSSE, with Astin's theory as the connective tissue.

DEV: That's the cleanest version. And the missing piece between them is the 1987 Chickering and Gamson "Seven Principles for Good Practice in Undergraduate Education." That gets distributed through the AAHE Bulletin, funded by Lilly Endowment, drafted at the Wingspread conference center. It takes Astin's theoretical claim and turns it into seven prescriptive principles — student-faculty contact, active learning, prompt feedback, time on task, high expectations, cooperation among students, respect for diverse talents and ways of learning. Those seven items are basically the prototype for what NSSE will measure a decade later.

MAYA: So by the time NSSE launches in '98, the theoretical work is fifteen years old and the measurement infrastructure is being built to give institutions a way to answer accountability questions without surrendering to the *U.S. News* rankings.

DEV: Exactly. Pew Charitable Trusts funds it. Peter Ewell at NCHEMS is in the design conversations. The whole project is a counter-instrument to selectivity-based rankings. Whether it succeeded in that mission is one of the live debates we'll get to.

[pause]

[THE CORE FRAMEWORK]

MAYA: Okay, can we make this concrete? Let's actually run a student through the framework.

DEV: Yes. Let's take a composite: a first-year student, call her Priya. She's commuting from home to a regional public university, working twenty-five hours a week off campus at a retail job, taking fifteen credits, and she's a first-generation student whose family expects her home for dinner most nights.

MAYA: So in Astin's original framework, Priya is — what — under-involved?

DEV: By the metrics Astin's residential-energy theory tracks, yes. She's not living on campus, she's working off campus rather than on campus — Astin's data shows on-campus employment is positively associated with persistence and off-campus negatively. She's probably not in clubs. She's not, in the language of the theory, devoting much physical and psychological energy to the academic experience compared to a residential student.

MAYA: But there's something obviously wrong with calling Priya under-involved. She's working three times harder than the residential student to be in college at all.

DEV: This is exactly the move that Astin's framework cannot make on its own. The theory measures energy directed at "the academic experience," which is functionally defined as on-campus academic and co-curricular activity. Energy directed at sustaining the conditions that make college possible — the retail job, the family caretaking — doesn't count as involvement. It's actually treated as competition for the finite energy that should be flowing toward the institution.

MAYA: Which is where the framework starts to look like it has an ideal student baked in.

DEV: Yes. And that ideal student is the residential, traditional-age, full-time student of CIRP's 1970s and '80s data. Astin's empirical base leaned heavily four-year, residential. Community colleges were outside it. Part-time students were marginal. By 1990, 43% of undergraduates were part-time. The theory was already in tension with the population by the time it was widely adopted.

Now — let's track Priya through what the framework can illuminate. Suppose she joins a learning community in her sophomore year, gets a campus job in the library, and starts going to office hours with a writing professor who notices her. According to Astin, three things are happening: she's increasing the quantity of involvement (more hours engaged with the institution), the quality is changing (the office hours are higher-quality involvement than passive class attendance), and the object of involvement is shifting (from purely transactional academic engagement to relational engagement with faculty).

MAYA: And then — the theory predicts what?

DEV: Better retention, better learning outcomes, better self-reported development. Which is what Astin's data shows. And what's important is that the theory predicts this without reference to Priya's prior preparation, her family background, or the institution's selectivity. The active ingredient is what she's now doing.

MAYA: Which is the egalitarian punch of the theory.

DEV: That's the punch. Astin says directly in the practical applications section that the content and resource models of pedagogy — the "great books" model, the "build a better library" model — quote, "tend to favor the well-prepared, assertive student." The involvement model, by contrast, focuses faculty attention on what every student is doing. It potentially levels.

MAYA: Potentially.

DEV: Potentially. Now the conceptual move from involvement to engagement happens when Kuh and the NSSE team operationalize it. NSSE doesn't measure "energy." You can't measure energy. So they measure proxies — frequency of student-faculty interaction, hours studying, participation in high-impact practices, engagement with diverse peers, perception of supportive campus environment. The original NSSE had five benchmarks; the 2013 redesign — what Kuh's team called NSSE 2.0 — replaced them with ten engagement indicators across four themes.

MAYA: And the high-impact practices framework — Kuh 2008 — turns this into a discrete list of activities. First-year seminars, learning communities, writing-intensive courses, undergraduate research, diversity learning, service learning, internships, capstones.

DEV: Right. HIPs become the institutional shorthand. If you can't measure energy, measure participation in the activities that the data show are correlated with the outcomes you care about. That's the operational logic.

MAYA: But there's a translation loss in there, right? Astin's theory was about a state — psychological energy. HIPs are about activities — sites where that state is more likely. We started measuring the sites and forgot the state.

DEV: That's exactly the translation loss. And it's worse than that, because the HIPs framework gets adopted as a checklist. Did the student do six of the eight? Are we offering all eight? The behavioral surface gets measured; the developmental depth recedes.

[pause]

[EVOLUTION AND REFINEMENTS]

MAYA: So if Astin's 1984 framework has these limits — the residential ideal-type, the measurement compression — what did the field do with that?

DEV: A few things happened in parallel. The first is internal refinement — Kuh and his collaborators kept tweaking the measurement instrument. The second is the high-impact practices consolidation, which we just talked about. The third — and this is where it gets intellectually serious — is critique from adjacent traditions. Let me name three.

One is Laura Rendón's validation theory, 1994 in *Innovative Higher Education*. Rendón's claim is that for nontraditional and minoritized students, the involvement-as-self-initiated-energy model fails. These students don't need to "get involved" in the Astinian sense — they need to be *validated*, often by an initiating act from a faculty member or staff member who reaches out, says you belong here, recognizes their cultural knowledge. The agentic direction is reversed.

MAYA: So Rendón is saying involvement assumes a student who already feels entitled to the institution.

DEV: Essentially yes. The energetic student of Astin's framework is one who has been culturally prepared to assume that exerting effort toward the institution will be reciprocated. That assumption isn't universal.

The second critique is Tara Yosso's 2005 piece in *Race Ethnicity and Education*, "Whose Culture Has Capital?" Yosso is working in Critical Race Theory, building on Ladson-Billings. Her move is to invert Bourdieu. Bourdieu argued that schools reproduce class advantage by treating dominant-culture capital as natural merit. Yosso says: communities of color possess six forms of capital — aspirational, navigational, social, linguistic, familial, resistant — that the dominant frameworks don't see as capital at all. Applied to engagement, the question becomes: whose practices count as engagement? If a student is engaging deeply in family caretaking, community organizing, code-switching across linguistic worlds — none of that shows up as involvement in an Astinian metric.

MAYA: So you can be a profoundly engaged person whose engagement is invisible to the instrument.

DEV: That's the charge. And the third critique — this is Samuel Museus's Culturally Engaging Campus Environments model, the CECE model, built across Museus & Jayakumar 2012 and consolidated in Museus 2017 in *Review of Higher Education* — moves the unit of analysis from student behavior to campus environment. Museus says, quote, "traditional student success frameworks, such as Tinto's integration theory, and the research that has employed them to study student success have focused more on students' behaviors than the environmental and psychological elements of college success." His CECE model has nine indicators — cultural familiarity, culturally relevant knowledge, cross-cultural engagement, collectivist cultural orientations, humanized educational environments, proactive philosophies, holistic support, and so on. The question isn't "are students involved?" It's "is this environment culturally engaging for the students who are actually here?"

MAYA: So is the original Astin framework still usable?

DEV: Yes and no. It's still empirically robust within its scope. The residential involvement findings have replicated for decades. Time on task still predicts learning outcomes. What's been refined is the scope. Astin's theory works best for residential, traditional-age, full-time students at four-year institutions — which is exactly the population CIRP was sampling. It needs supplementation, not abandonment, for other populations.

What the field has done less well is acknowledge that the supplementation isn't a small patch. If you accept the CECE critique, you've essentially relocated the active ingredient from student energy to institutional environment. That's not an extension of Astin. That's a different theory wearing a similar vocabulary.

MAYA: There's also the cognitive-developmental track running alongside this, right? Baxter Magolda, King.

DEV: Yes — and this is where the picture gets more complicated. Marcia Baxter Magolda's longitudinal work, especially *Making Their Own Way* in 2001, develops the construct of self-authorship from Kegan's fourth-order consciousness. She tracks participants from their undergraduate years through their twenties. Her three dimensions are — quote — "the epistemological dimension," how we know or decide what to believe; "the intrapersonal dimension," how we view ourselves; and "the interpersonal dimension," how we construct relationships with others. She argues these are intertwined and that the integration of all three is self-authorship.

What's important here for our concept is that Baxter Magolda is essentially asking: involvement in *what*, toward *what end*? The behavioral measurement of NSSE doesn't tell you whether the student is developing the capacity to internally generate beliefs, identity, and relationships, or whether they're just doing the things engaged students do without changing how they make meaning.

MAYA: So engagement could be developmentally hollow.

DEV: It could be. A student can rack up high-impact practice participation, faculty interaction frequency, hours of study — and still be operating on what Baxter Magolda calls "following external formulas." High behavior, no transformation.

[LIVE DEBATES]

MAYA: Where is the concept contested right now? Not generically — specifically.

DEV: Three places I'd name. First: whether engagement is a property of students or of environments. Astin located it in the student. Museus and the CECE tradition locate it in the environment. NSSE straddles — it measures student-reported behaviors but uses them as institutional indicators. The unresolved question is whose responsibility it is when engagement is low, and that matters because it determines what gets fixed and who gets blamed.

MAYA: Push back on that — isn't that an easy synthesis? Both matter, interaction effect, done.

DEV: It looks like an easy synthesis until you think about resource allocation. If engagement is mostly student behavior, you fund advising, orientation, retention coaching — interventions on the student. If engagement is mostly environmental, you fund faculty development, curricular redesign, institutional culture change — interventions on the institution. Real budgets get made in one direction or the other.

MAYA: Okay, fair.

DEV: Second debate: whether the engagement framework can survive the demographic and structural reality of contemporary higher education. The theoretical machinery assumes a residential, full-time, co-present student body. By the late 2000s, online enrollment was over 3.9 million students per the Sloan Consortium. After COVID, hybrid and online enrollment is permanent. What does psychological energy devoted to the academic experience mean when the academic experience is asynchronous and mediated through an LMS?

MAYA: NSSE has adapted to that — they have online versions, they've added items.

DEV: They have. But the deeper question is whether the construct still does the work. The original theory was built on co-presence. Eating, sleeping, and spending waking hours on campus, in Astin's own words. Strip out the co-presence and the theory has to be reconstructed, not just rescaled.

Third debate: what I'd call the domestication problem. The recurring pattern is that critical frameworks get absorbed into the engagement apparatus by being turned into measurement. Yosso's community cultural wealth gets cited extensively, but NSSE adds a few items and the underlying frame holds. CECE itself is moving down the same path — to be institutionally adopted it had to take a benchmark form, with measurable indicators and institutional applications. Which is the same form that domesticated Astin's developmental theory into an accountability instrument.

MAYA: And you're saying that's bad?

DEV: I'm saying it's the tension. The institutional uptake requires measurement. Measurement requires translation. Translation flattens. And the question is whether there's any other route to influence in U.S. higher education, given how the accountability infrastructure is built. Maybe there isn't. But the cost is that critical frameworks lose their edge when they become NSSE indicators.

MAYA: Let me push back here. You're presenting this as a tragedy of co-optation, but isn't the alternative — being intellectually pure but institutionally invisible — actually worse? Yosso's framework has reshaped how a generation of student affairs practitioners think about asset-based work. That's not nothing.

DEV: That's a fair pushback. And I'd grant most of it. The asset-based vocabulary is genuinely more humane than the deficit framing it replaced. But there's a difference between vocabulary change and structural change. The deficit framing is now spoken about less; the measurement apparatus that encoded it is still largely in place. The risk is mistaking the first for the second.

MAYA: Okay. Where do you land?

DEV: I land on: Astin's involvement theory was a real intellectual achievement that has been institutionally over-extended. The original claim — student behavior matters more than institutional characteristics — was a useful corrective. The thirty-year afterlife of that claim, in which "engagement" becomes the dominant operational vocabulary of student affairs, has flattened the theory and exported its assumptions to populations and contexts where they don't hold. The corrective now is to insist on the conditions under which the original claim was true.

[pause]

[WHAT THE FIELD GETS WRONG]

MAYA: Two-minute version: what does the field routinely get wrong about this?

DEV: The biggest misreading is treating high-impact practices as a checklist rather than as a hypothesis. Kuh's 2008 framework lists eight practices that the data show are correlated with positive outcomes — especially, importantly, for underserved students. The field hears: do these eight things. What Kuh actually argued is that these practices share underlying features — they demand significant time and effort, they require interaction with faculty and peers about substantive matters, they place students in unfamiliar circumstances, they involve frequent feedback, they integrate learning across contexts. The active ingredients are the features, not the activities.

MAYA: So you can offer a service-learning course that has none of those features.

DEV: You can offer a service-learning course that's a checklist item with no feedback, no substantive faculty interaction, no integration. It counts as a HIP. It does nothing. Whereas a regular philosophy seminar with intensive feedback, faculty engagement, and integration with other coursework has all the active ingredients and isn't on the list.

The second misreading is treating "involvement" and "engagement" as synonyms. They're not. Involvement is Astin's theoretical construct about energy. Engagement is the operational successor that turned the theoretical construct into measurable behavior. When practitioners say "engaged students," they usually mean "students who do the things engaged students do" — which is a tautology that has lost the developmental claim Astin was trying to make.

MAYA: And the third misreading?

DEV: That engagement is a content-neutral, ideologically quiet construct. It isn't. It was politically attractive in the late 1980s and '90s partly *because* it appeared neutral — measurable, behavioral, indifferent to what students were engaging with. That apparent neutrality is itself a substantive position. It says the form of educational labor matters, the content doesn't. That's a real claim, and it's one the culture wars made attractive to administrators. Pretending it's just a technical construct misses what it's doing politically.

[IMPLICATIONS FOR YOUR WORK]

MAYA: Okay, let me bring this home. If I'm a doctoral student, a student affairs practitioner, an administrator — what does this episode change about Monday morning?

For dissertation researchers: if you're studying engagement, do not treat NSSE scores as a measurement of Astin's construct. They aren't. NSSE measures self-reported behavioral proxies under specific conditions. If you want to measure something closer to Astin's psychological-energy claim, you need observational, qualitative, or experience-sampling data. The instruments you have access to and the construct you think you're measuring are not the same thing, and the methods chapter has to acknowledge that.

DEV: And if you're studying minoritized students specifically, you have to read Yosso, Rendón, and Museus alongside Astin. Not as supplementary critique — as primary frameworks. The CECE indicators may fit your population better than NSSE indicators do.

MAYA: For student affairs practitioners: stop running involvement campaigns that assume your students have the spare capacity to be involved. The "get involved" framing is built for the residential traditional-age student who has time and energy unallocated. For a working, commuting, first-generation student, the question is not "how do we get her involved?" but "how does this institution validate the engagement she's already doing?" That's a Rendón move, and it has different programmatic implications. It means initiating contact rather than waiting for students to seek you out. It means embedding engagement opportunities into required academic structures rather than offering them as optional add-ons.

DEV: For faculty: the active ingredient in Astin's framework is faculty-student interaction of substantive quality. Office hours that no one attends are an empty signal. Building substantive interaction into the structure of the course — required conferences, low-stakes feedback cycles, peer interaction with faculty mediation — is the real lever. The seven principles from Chickering and Gamson are still a workable checklist for course design, and they're more useful than the HIPs list because they're features, not activities.

MAYA: For administrators: the question to ask about any engagement intervention is not "are we offering it?" but "for whom is it accessible, and what conditions make it actually transformational rather than ceremonial?" A first-year seminar can be a HIP or a hollow ritual. A learning community can be a substantive integrating experience or a scheduling convenience. The label tells you nothing.

DEV: And institutionally — for those of you in IR offices or assessment offices — the gap between NSSE indicators and the actual developmental claims of the theory is your gap to bridge. Triangulate. Don't let one survey carry the weight of an entire educational philosophy.

MAYA: One more from me. For anyone advising graduate students writing on this topic: do not let them cite "Astin (1984)" without reading the actual fourteen pages. The article is shorter than most of the secondary literature about it, and it contains qualifications and nuances that have been lost in the citation chain.

[pause]

[READING ROADMAP]

DEV: Four texts, in order.

First: Astin 1984. Read the actual article. Fourteen pages. It will surprise you with what it claims and what it doesn't. The continuum claim, the qualitative dimension, the practical applications section where Astin himself names the limits of the framework — none of that survives in the secondary citation life of the piece.

Second: Pascarella and Terenzini's *How College Affects Students*, 1991 — and the 2005 second volume by Pascarella and Terenzini, then the 2016 third volume edited by Mayhew, Rockenbach, Bowman, and others. This is the comprehensive synthesis that canonized Astin alongside Tinto, Chickering, and Pace. You read it second because it gives you the empirical context — what the involvement claims look like in the aggregated data — that the original article asserts but doesn't show.

Third: Kuh's *High-Impact Educational Practices*, 2008, the AAC&U LEAP publication. This is the operational translation. Read it after Astin and Pascarella because you'll see what happens when a theoretical claim becomes an institutional product. You'll see what's preserved and what's lost in the translation.

Fourth: Museus 2017 in *Review of Higher Education*, the CECE article. This is the reconstruction. Read it last because you need the prior three to see what Museus is doing — what he keeps from the involvement and integration traditions, what he relocates, what he refuses. Read alongside it Yosso 2005 if you have time for a fifth, because Museus is building on Yosso explicitly.

If you want to add the developmental layer — Baxter Magolda's *Making Their Own Way* belongs in the conversation, but it's a different lineage. Read it when you're ready to ask what engagement is supposed to be *for*, developmentally. That's the question the engagement literature largely doesn't answer.

[pause]

[WHAT WE DON'T KNOW]

MAYA: One genuinely open question.

DEV: Here's the one I'd name. We do not know what engagement means in an educational environment where significant portions of cognitive labor can be outsourced to generative AI. The construct of psychological energy devoted to the academic experience was built for an environment in which the student's cognitive effort and the academic product were tightly coupled. Strip out that coupling — when a student can produce a competent paper with thirty seconds of prompting — and the entire behavioral surface of engagement decouples from the developmental substance it was supposed to indicate. A student can show every NSSE behavior, complete every HIP, log every interaction, and have done none of the cognitive work the theory assumed was happening underneath. We don't yet have a theory of engagement adequate to that condition. We're going to need one within the next five years, and the field has barely started building it.
