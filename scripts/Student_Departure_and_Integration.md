# Scholar Stacks — Student Departure and Integration

[COLD OPEN]

DEV: There's a strange thing about Vincent Tinto's 1975 paper. It's published in *Review of Educational Research*. It's about why students drop out of college. And the framework it imports — the structural backbone of the whole thing — is Émile Durkheim's 1897 theory of suicide. A French sociologist explaining nineteenth-century death rates becomes the engine for explaining why a kid from Buffalo doesn't come back to Syracuse after winter break. And that move, that translation, ends up being the most cited theoretical framework in the history of higher education research.

MAYA: Right, and I want to flag something before we get into it. I was on a retention committee last semester. Somebody pulled up a dashboard — academic integration scores, social integration scores, predicted persistence — and presented it like it was meteorology. Like we were reading a weather map. Nobody on that committee said the word Durkheim. Nobody said Van Gennep. The framework had become invisible infrastructure. So part of what I want from this episode is — how did a sociological theory with very specific assumptions get turned into a piece of administrative software that nobody questions anymore?

DEV: And the deeper question underneath that: when scholars of color started saying, in the early nineties, that this model was demanding cultural assimilation as the price of academic survival, why did the model survive the critique? Why is Tinto still in the textbooks? That's the puzzle.

MAYA: Okay. Let's go.

[pause]

[INTELLECTUAL ORIGINS]

DEV: So Tinto's 1975 piece — "Dropout from Higher Education: A Theoretical Synthesis of Recent Research" — does not come out of nowhere. The direct predecessor is William Spady. Spady publishes in *Interchange* in 1970, and Spady is the one who first explicitly says: let's take Durkheim's *Suicide* and use it to think about students leaving college. Spady's the one who lays the rail. Tinto comes along five years later and builds the train.

MAYA: Why does Tinto eclipse Spady? Because Spady got there first.

DEV: Two reasons. One, Tinto turns it into a longitudinal process model with measurable constructs. Spady has the structural intuition; Tinto operationalizes it. He gives you goal commitment, institutional commitment, academic integration, social integration — things you can put on a survey instrument. Two, Tinto writes the 1987 book, *Leaving College*, which translates the theory into a book that an enrollment management VP can actually read. Spady stays an article; Tinto becomes a book and then a second edition in 1993.

MAYA: Wait — why did anyone need a new concept here? Like, what was the field doing about dropouts in 1970 that was so insufficient?

DEV: This is the part I think gets lost. The sociology of education in the late sixties and early seventies is dominated by status attainment models. Blau and Duncan in 1967. The Wisconsin Model — Sewell and Hauser. These are basically input-output regressions. You take family background, parental education, test scores, you regress them onto educational attainment, and the institution is a black box. The student goes in, the student comes out, what happens inside doesn't theoretically matter.

MAYA: So Tinto's move is to crack open the black box.

DEV: Exactly. He inserts institutional process between input and output. He says: it's not just who you are when you arrive. It's what happens to you on campus — specifically, do you get woven into the academic system and the social system of this place. And that interactionist move is what makes the model feel new. It treats the institution as a setting that acts on the student rather than a neutral container the student passes through.

MAYA: And the historical context here matters, right? Because 1970 to 1975 is also when higher ed is changing demographically in ways the old models can't handle.

DEV: Hugely. The Education Amendments of 1972 create what become Pell Grants. Enrollment goes from about 8 million in 1970 to 12 million by 1980. CUNY goes open admissions in 1970. You have community colleges absorbing forty percent of undergrads by the end of the decade. The residential, selective, four-year college that the prior research had implicitly described is no longer the typical case. And administrators are panicking — the Carnegie Commission under Clark Kerr is putting out reports forecasting a demographic cliff. Retention becomes an institutional survival problem before it becomes a research problem.

MAYA: So the field needs a theory that does two things at once. It needs to be intellectually respectable — sociologically grounded — and it needs to give administrators something to do on Monday morning.

DEV: That's the Tinto sweet spot. Now the second source — and this is the one Tierney is going to detonate twenty years later — is Arnold van Gennep, the French ethnographer who wrote *Les rites de passage* in 1909. Translated into English in 1960. Van Gennep's three-stage schema is separation, transition, incorporation. The initiate separates from a prior status, passes through a liminal phase, and is incorporated into a new status. Tinto uses this as the developmental arc of the college experience. You separate from your home community, you go through a transitional first year, and you get incorporated into the institution.

MAYA: I want to flag what I think is going to be the problem here. Van Gennep is describing rites of passage *inside* a community. A boy becomes a man in his own village. He doesn't leave one culture and join a different one.

DEV: That's exactly the critique Tierney makes in 1992. Hold it. We'll come back to it. The other thing to note is that Tinto's framework lands in 1975 alongside Alexander Astin's *Preventing Students from Dropping Out* — same year. And Astin is building something parallel: the input-environment-output model, which becomes the involvement theory by 1984. Astin and Tinto coexist uneasily for two decades. They never really polemicize at each other. But there's a real intellectual difference.

MAYA: What's the difference?

DEV: Astin is more behavioral and more psychological. His 1984 paper in *Journal of College Student Personnel* — which is in our corpus — defines involvement as the investment of physical and psychic energy. And his evidence is striking. He writes, quote, "Living in a campus residence was positively related to retention, and this positive effect occurred in all types of institutions and among all types of students regardless of sex, race, ability, or family background." End quote. That's a remarkable empirical claim. Residence trumps demographics.

MAYA: That's the kind of finding that gets a residential-life budget approved.

DEV: Right, and Astin's framework is more parsimonious. It's basically: time on task in the institution predicts outcomes. Tinto's is more structural — it's about systems of integration, not just hours of engagement. But they share an assumption, and this is what later critics will hammer: engagement with the institution as it currently exists is the relevant predictor. Neither framework, in its original form, asks whether the institution is worth being engaged with for everyone.

[pause]

[THE CORE FRAMEWORK]

MAYA: Okay. Walk me through what the model actually says. Pretend I'm a first-year doctoral student who has to explain this on a qualifying exam.

DEV: The framework is longitudinal. It moves through time. You start with pre-entry attributes — family background, prior schooling, individual attributes like ability. Those attributes shape initial goal commitment, which is how committed are you to the goal of getting a degree, and initial institutional commitment, which is how committed are you to *this* institution specifically.

MAYA: Those are separate variables on purpose.

DEV: On purpose. Because a student can be highly committed to getting a degree but ambivalent about this particular campus, and that combination predicts transfer, not dropout. Or you can be committed to the campus — you love the football team, your friends are here — but ambivalent about the degree, and that predicts a different kind of departure. Tinto separates these because they have different downstream behaviors.

MAYA: Okay.

DEV: Then the student enters institutional experiences, which Tinto divides into two systems. The academic system is formal — courses, grades, faculty interactions in academic settings — and informal — intellectual development, identification with academic values. The social system is also formal — extracurricular activities — and informal — peer-group interactions. Out of those experiences come two integration outcomes: academic integration and social integration. Those then feed back to revise your goal and institutional commitments. And those revised commitments predict your departure decision.

MAYA: Let me try to ground this. I'll give you a student. Marisol. First-generation, from a Spanish-speaking household in the Central Valley. She enrolls at a four-year public in another part of the state. She's a strong student academically — pulls a 3.6 her first semester. But she's commuting an hour each way to save on housing, she's working twenty-five hours a week at a restaurant, and she doesn't really know anybody on campus.

DEV: So in Tinto's terms, you'd say her academic integration is reasonably strong — the grades are a proxy for that — but her social integration is thin. She has no peer network on campus, she's not in clubs, she's not eating in the dining hall. The model predicts that even with strong academic performance, the weak social integration is going to erode her institutional commitment over time. She'll start questioning whether this particular place is worth the commute. And departure becomes likely — not because she can't do the work, but because she never got woven into the social fabric.

MAYA: Okay but here's where I want to push. The model treats her thin social integration as a problem in her relationship to the campus. But the actual constraint is that she's working twenty-five hours a week because tuition went up, and her family needs her income. The "social integration deficit" is a downstream effect of a structural condition. The model is describing the symptom and naming it the cause.

DEV: That is exactly the critique. Hold that thought because it's going to come back. But within the model's own logic, you would say: the institution can intervene by creating low-cost, low-time-commitment forms of social integration — a commuter student lounge, a learning community that meets during a class period she's already taking, faculty office hours that double as social space. The model gives you intervention points. That's why administrators love it.

MAYA: And the Durkheim piece is — what — the integration into the moral community?

DEV: Durkheim in 1897 argues that suicide rates vary inversely with integration into social collectives. Too little integration produces what he calls egoistic suicide. The individual is not woven into the moral and affiliative bonds of a group. Tinto takes that structural insight — integration as a predictor of withdrawal — and applies it to college leaving. Academic integration in Tinto maps roughly onto Durkheim's moral integration; social integration maps onto affiliative integration.

MAYA: What gets lost in translation?

DEV: Two things, and they matter. First, Durkheim's argument is that integration is morally ambivalent. Too little is dangerous — egoistic suicide — but so is too much, which he calls altruistic suicide. The kamikaze pilot, the martyr. Tinto's model treats integration as monotonically good. More is always better. The Durkheimian U-curve becomes a straight line.

MAYA: So the model loses Durkheim's ambivalence.

DEV: Loses the ambivalence. Second, with Van Gennep, what gets lost is the cultural neutrality of the rite. Van Gennep describes rites that bring an initiate more deeply into their own community. Tinto inverts this. The student is separating from their home community and being incorporated into the institution's culture. That asymmetry is going to be the whole problem.

MAYA: Is there a place in Tinto where he explicitly says the student has to break from home?

DEV: In the 1987 book he talks about needing to "disassociate" from prior memberships as part of the separation stage. The Museus 2014 piece in our corpus paraphrases this directly. Museus writes that critics "have questioned the underlying cultural foundations of Tinto's theory, which suggest that students must dissociate from" — and that paraphrase is doing accurate work. Tinto really does say that.

MAYA: So the framework gives Marisol two options. Stay tethered to her family and community and risk departure. Or detach from them and integrate. Those are the choices the model offers.

DEV: That's the binary it constructs. And that's the binary the cultural critique is going to refuse.

[pause]

[EVOLUTION AND REFINEMENTS]

DEV: So 1992. William Tierney publishes "An Anthropological Analysis of Student Participation in College" in the *Journal of Higher Education*. Tierney is trained as an anthropologist, he's at USC, he eventually anchors the Pullias Center. And the move he makes is technical and devastating at the same time.

MAYA: What's the technical part?

DEV: He goes back to Van Gennep and says Tinto misread the source. Van Gennep was describing rites of passage within a culture. The initiate doesn't change cultures; they change status within their own culture. Tinto applied the structure to a cross-cultural transition — student leaving home culture, entering institutional culture — and that's a category error. What Tinto is describing isn't a rite of passage. It's assimilation. And demanding that minoritized students assimilate to a dominant institutional culture in order to persist is not a developmental theory. It's a coercive theory dressed in developmental language.

MAYA: How does Tinto respond?

DEV: He partially concedes in the 1993 revision. He acknowledges cultural difference. He adds qualifications. But he keeps the integration architecture. And here's what's revealing — the retention industry domesticates the critique. They add "culturally responsive" programming on top of an unchanged model. The model itself doesn't move.

MAYA: That's where I want to push back a little. Because it sounds like you're saying Tinto was just stubborn. But isn't it possible the core mechanism — that institutional belonging predicts persistence — is genuinely robust, and what needed updating was the cultural content, not the mechanism?

DEV: That's actually the most charitable defense of Tinto and it's worth taking seriously. You can argue: the predictive structure — that students who feel embedded in the institution persist at higher rates than those who don't — is empirically replicated across every demographic group. What's contested is what produces that embedding. Tinto assumed it required cultural conformity. Subsequent scholars argue it can be produced through cultural validation instead. Same outcome variable, different causal pathway.

MAYA: Okay, that's clearer.

DEV: The other major extension comes from Laura Rendón. Her 1994 *Innovative Higher Education* piece introduces validation theory. Rendón is doing focus groups with Latina students at community colleges, and what she finds is that these students don't persist because they integrate. They persist because someone — a faculty member, a staff member, often a custodial worker — validates them as capable college students. The mechanism is affirmation from a person with institutional standing, not assimilation into a culture.

MAYA: That's a really different causal story.

DEV: It's a different causal story. And it sets up Tara Yosso, 2005, "Whose Culture Has Capital?" in *Race Ethnicity and Education*. Yosso is working from Critical Race Theory and LatCrit. Her target is Bourdieu's cultural capital concept and the way integration models read minoritized students as lacking what institutions require. Yosso flips it. She says communities of color possess six forms of capital: aspirational, navigational, social, linguistic, familial, and resistant. These aren't compensation for what's missing. They're real capital that the institution fails to recognize.

MAYA: So the deficit framing isn't a description of the students. It's a description of what the institution counts.

DEV: That's the move. "Calling students underprepared is a category error produced by what the researcher counts." That's the Yosso argument.

MAYA: So is the original Tinto version still usable? Like, if I'm writing a dissertation in 2024, can I use it?

DEV: I'd say yes with conditions. The model is descriptively useful in residential, four-year, racially homogenous settings — which is to say, the settings it was developed in. It performs poorly when applied to community college students, commuter students, transfer students, adult learners, and students of color in PWIs. Which is most undergraduates now. So it's a special-case theory that the field treated as a general theory.

[pause]

[LIVE DEBATES]

DEV: The biggest live debate is one Samuel Museus crystallizes. His 2014 CECE chapter in the Smart series — the *Higher Education: Handbook of Theory and Research* — explicitly stages the disagreement. Let me quote him: "traditional student success frameworks, such as Tinto's (1987, 1993) integration theory, and the research that has employed them to study student success have focused more on students' behaviors than the environmental and psychological elements of college success." End quote.

MAYA: So Museus is saying — the unit of analysis is wrong. We've been measuring students when we should be measuring environments.

DEV: That's the directionality flip. Museus's CECE model — Culturally Engaging Campus Environments — is built around nine indicators. Cultural familiarity, culturally relevant knowledge, cultural community service, meaningful cross-cultural engagement, collectivist cultural orientations, culturally validating environments, humanized educational environments, proactive philosophies, and holistic support. These are properties of the campus, not of the student.

MAYA: And belonging replaces integration as the mediating variable.

DEV: Yes. And that semantic move matters. "Sense of belonging" came out of Hurtado and Carter's 1997 paper. It's been picked up by Terrell Strayhorn in his book on Black male belonging. Museus uses belonging because it doesn't carry the assimilationist freight that integration carries.

MAYA: But here's a debate I want to surface — is sense of belonging actually different from integration? Or is it the same construct with a less politically loaded label?

DEV: That is exactly the second live debate. The skeptics — and there are serious quantitative scholars who hold this position — say if you correlate belonging scales with integration scales, you get correlations above point seven. They're measuring substantially overlapping constructs. The relabeling has been domesticated by the practice it critiqued.

MAYA: What's the Museus camp's response to that?

DEV: That the predictors of belonging are different. Belonging-via-cultural-engagement is produced by different institutional inputs than integration-via-assimilation. Even if the outcome variable looks similar, the causal pathway is structurally different. And practically, the interventions look different. A Tinto-informed intervention asks how do we get this student more involved in our existing structures. A Museus-informed intervention asks how do we restructure our environment so that it engages this student's cultural background.

MAYA: I want to push on you here. You sound like you're more sympathetic to Museus.

DEV: I am.

MAYA: But Museus is also using regression models, sense-of-belonging scales, statistical significance at the point-oh-one level — the table in our corpus is literally a regression table. Hasn't he absorbed the methodological apparatus of the very tradition he's critiquing? If Yosso's community cultural wealth was supposed to be a counter-story, not a measurement instrument, hasn't CECE captured the counter-story for quantitative respectability?

DEV: That's the third live debate, and it's the QuantCrit critique — Gillborn, Warmington, Demack, 2018. They argue that operationalizing CRT-grounded frameworks into scales strips them of their critical edge. The instruments end up measurable, which means they end up dashboarded, which means they end up in an early-alert system, which is exactly the bureaucratic apparatus that produced the original problem. Museus would say you have to play the empirical game to be taken seriously in tenure-track higher ed scholarship. The QuantCrit critics would say that game has rules that make the critique impossible to win.

MAYA: That's a real tension. I don't think it's resolvable.

DEV: I don't think it's resolvable either, and I think anyone studying retention in 2024 has to pick a position on it. Where I land — and this is contested — is that the measurement-versus-counter-story split is itself a false binary. You can run validated regressions on environmental indicators while also doing the counter-story work. They're different products for different audiences. The danger is when the regression eats the narrative.

MAYA: The third critique that's live — and I haven't heard you address it yet — is Estela Bensimon's equity-mindedness work at USC. Bensimon basically refuses the whole student-centered frame. She says: stop asking what's wrong with the students who don't persist. Audit the institution. Build an equity scorecard. Hold practitioners accountable for racially disaggregated outcomes.

DEV: That's the most structural position in the debate. And it's the one that's least compatible with Tinto's framework because it doesn't accept the integration outcome as the thing to optimize. It says even if students are integrated, if the disaggregated graduation rates show racial inequity, the institution has failed. Integration is not the metric; equity is.

[pause]

[WHAT THE FIELD GETS WRONG]

MAYA: Okay. The misreading.

DEV: When practitioners cite Tinto — and they cite him constantly — they almost always cite the academic-and-social integration piece and ignore the goal-commitment piece. They treat the model as if its prescription is "get students more involved." That is not actually what Tinto's model claims.

MAYA: Say more.

DEV: The model's claim is that integration *modifies* goal and institutional commitment, and the *commitments* predict departure. Integration is mediated. It's not the proximal cause. If a student has very low goal commitment to begin with — they're not sure they want a degree at all — making them more socially integrated does very little. They'll have a great time and still leave. Conversely, a student with very high goal commitment can persist through low social integration if their institutional commitment stays intact.

MAYA: So the field has flattened a longitudinal mediation model into a single-variable prescription.

DEV: That's the flattening. And the prescription gets even worse. Practitioners read "social integration" as "join a club." Tinto's social integration includes informal peer interactions, value congruence with the institution, identification with the social fabric. A club is one possible site for that. It's not the construct. But the predictive analytics systems often code club membership as the proxy variable because it's the data the registrar has.

MAYA: So you can have a high integration score by Tinto's actual definition and a low integration score by the system's measurement, or vice versa.

DEV: All the time. And the second misreading is even more consequential. The field treats Tinto's model as if it's a theory of student deficit. As if it's saying: students fail to integrate because of something about them. Tinto's 1993 revision is actually explicit that institutional fit matters and that institutions have responsibilities. The crude application of the model in early-alert systems strips that out. The systems flag students; they don't flag institutional patterns. The framework's institutional half has been amputated by its software implementations.

[pause]

[IMPLICATIONS FOR YOUR WORK]

MAYA: I want to translate this. If you're a doctoral student writing a dissertation on retention right now, what do you actually do with this?

DEV: Three things. First, name your framework explicitly. Don't cite Tinto as if he's neutral background. He's a contested theoretical position. If you're using academic and social integration as variables, say what you're inheriting and say what you're not. The unmarked Tinto citation is over.

MAYA: Second?

DEV: Second, think hard about your dependent variable. If you're using six-year graduation, you're using the IPEDS metric, which was created by Student Right-to-Know in 1990 and which assumes a first-time, full-time, single-institution trajectory. That metric encodes the residential-college assumption. If you're studying community college students or transfer students, you need a dependent variable that doesn't pre-assume the trajectory the metric was designed to measure. Doug Shapiro's work at the National Student Clearinghouse has better cross-institutional metrics. Use them.

MAYA: Third?

DEV: Third, take environmental measurement seriously. Museus's CECE scale is validated. Hurtado's campus racial climate instruments are validated. If you're doing a quantitative dissertation, you don't have to choose between Tinto's variables and structural critique. You can include both and let the data tell you which carries more predictive weight in your population.

MAYA: For practitioners — student affairs folks, advisors, retention specialists — what changes Monday morning?

DEV: Stop asking what's wrong with the student. Start asking what the student's interaction with the institution is producing. The Marisol example we did earlier — the Tinto-informed advisor says, you need to get more involved on campus, here are some clubs. The validation-informed or CECE-informed advisor says, what would it take for the time you're already on campus to feel like a place you belong? Maybe that means putting her in a learning community with other commuter students. Maybe that means a faculty member who speaks Spanish and can validate her bilingualism as an academic asset rather than treating it as a remediation problem.

MAYA: And for administrators — deans, VPs of student affairs, provosts.

DEV: Audit the assumptions in your retention infrastructure. Pull up the predictive model your vendor sold you and ask: what variables go in? What's the outcome? Whose theory is encoded in this? If the variables are all student behaviors and none are institutional patterns, you've bought Tinto without the institutional half. The model is doing something narrower than what the original theory claimed, and you're making decisions based on the narrower thing without knowing it.

MAYA: One more — for faculty.

DEV: For faculty, the implication is that you are a site of integration or validation. Tinto's informal academic system runs through your office hours. Rendón's validation runs through whether you affirm a student as a capable college thinker. You don't need to know the literature to do that work. But it helps to know that when a student doesn't come back, it's rarely about ability. It's about whether the institution gave them a reason to believe they were a member of it.

[pause]

[READING ROADMAP]

DEV: Four texts in order. First, Tinto's 1975 *Review of Educational Research* article. Not the 1987 book. The article. It's tighter, the sociological scaffolding is more visible, and you can see the Durkheim and Spady moves unmasked. Read it for the theoretical architecture, not the empirics.

Second, Tierney's 1992 *Journal of Higher Education* piece, "An Anthropological Analysis of Student Participation in College." Read this immediately after Tinto so you can feel the impact of the Van Gennep critique while Tinto's framework is still fresh in your head. The critique loses force if you read it years later as a footnote.

Third, Yosso's 2005 "Whose Culture Has Capital?" in *Race Ethnicity and Education*. This is what gives you the affirmative alternative — the language of community cultural wealth. You need this before you read Museus because Museus is in part operationalizing Yosso.

Fourth, Museus's 2014 CECE chapter in the Smart *Handbook* series. Read this last because it's where you see what a successor framework looks like when it absorbs both the critique and the empirical apparatus. It's the synthesis that lets you understand how this debate sits in the field right now.

If you have a fifth slot, add Bensimon's equity-mindedness work — the 2005 *Change* essay is a good entry — for the structural position that refuses the student-centered frame entirely.

[pause]

[WHAT WE DON'T KNOW]

MAYA: Open question.

DEV: Here's the one I don't think the field has answered. We know that institutions vary enormously in their cultural engagement. We know that student demographics vary. We know that integration, belonging, and validation all predict persistence in various studies. What we don't know is whether the predictive weight of cultural engagement holds when the institution itself is in crisis — when it's closing programs, laying off faculty, watching enrollment collapse. The CECE model and the integration model were both developed in a period of relative institutional stability. The "some college, no credential" population is now thirty-six million people. Small private colleges are closing. State DEI offices are being legislated out of existence. Does the relationship between environmental cultural engagement and student persistence survive in a contracting, contested sector? Or are we describing the dynamics of a higher education system that no longer exists? I don't know. And I don't think anyone has the data yet to know.

MAYA: That's the question.
