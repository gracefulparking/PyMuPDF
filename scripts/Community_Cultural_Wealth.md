# Scholar Stacks — Community Cultural Wealth

**COLD OPEN**

DEV: Here's the thing that should bother us. In 2005, Tara Yosso publishes an article in *Race Ethnicity and Education* — twenty-some pages, a fairly compact theoretical move, not an empirical study with new data. Within a decade, "community cultural wealth" is in dissertation chapters, strategic plans, grant proposals, NASPA conference programs, the names of fellowship programs. The Taylor & Francis page logs something like thirty-eight thousand views and counting. And in that same period, you can read article after article that cites Yosso, lists the six capitals, and proceeds to do research that she would probably find recognizable as the very deficit thinking she was trying to dismantle.

MAYA: That's the puzzle I keep running into in my own work. I had a meeting last week with an administrator who said, completely sincerely, "we're using a community cultural wealth framework this year." And what she meant was, the orientation program is going to ask first-gen students to share their family stories. Which — fine. But that's not what the article says.

DEV: Right. So the question for this episode is not just "what is community cultural wealth." It's: how does a critique get domesticated by the very field it was trying to indict, and what does that tell us about how higher ed metabolizes critical scholarship?

MAYA: And whether the framework can do the work it was built to do, in 2025, in a policy environment that didn't exist when Yosso wrote it.

[pause]

**INTELLECTUAL ORIGINS**

DEV: To understand where the 2005 piece comes from, you have to understand a quarrel between two intellectual traditions that were both running hot in the late 1990s. The European, structuralist side is Pierre Bourdieu. The American, racial-justice side is Critical Race Theory, which by then had been in education for a full decade.

MAYA: Start with Bourdieu, because I think people use his name without quite knowing what he argued.

DEV: Bourdieu, writing in French sociology of education in the 70s and 80s — the key essay for U.S. audiences is "The Forms of Capital," translated into English in 1986 in John Richardson's handbook — argued that schools reproduce class hierarchies by treating the cultural repertoire of dominant classes as if it were natural intelligence. Particular linguistic codes, aesthetic preferences, familiarity with art and music and credentialing rituals — schools reward these and call the rewarding "merit." It's a diagnostic, structural critique. Schools are not neutral evaluators of talent; they are sorting mechanisms that launder class advantage into academic achievement.

MAYA: So Bourdieu is fundamentally about indicting the institution.

DEV: Exactly. And here's what happened when U.S. education researchers imported him through the 1980s and 90s — and Patricia McDonough's 1997 book *Choosing Colleges* is one of the cleaner higher-ed examples. The structural critique translated. But the relational definition of capital — the idea that what counts as capital depends on the field that confers value — got flattened. "Cultural capital" became a property checklist. Some students had it. Some didn't. The ones who didn't were the ones whose families didn't read to them, didn't take them to museums, didn't speak standard English at home.

MAYA: Which inverts the whole point.

DEV: Completely. Bourdieu's critique of schools became, in American hands, a deficit inventory of families. And here's Yosso's framing of this, almost word for word from the 2005 piece — she writes that "while Bourdieu's work sought to provide a structural critique of social and cultural reproduction, his theory of cultural capital has been used to assert that some communities are culturally" — and the sentence continues into the deficit move — wealthier than others.

MAYA: So that's stream one. What's stream two?

DEV: Critical Race Theory, which is born in legal studies in the 1970s and 80s — Derrick Bell, Kimberlé Crenshaw, Richard Delgado, Patricia Williams. The conventional date for its migration into education is Gloria Ladson-Billings and William Tate's 1995 article in *Teachers College Record*, "Toward a Critical Race Theory of Education." That piece argued that race was undertheorized in education research and that the field needed CRT's commitments — to counter-storytelling, to the experiential knowledge of people of color as legitimate evidence, to a critique of liberal colorblindness.

MAYA: And Yosso's training sits inside that lineage directly.

DEV: She's a Daniel Solórzano student at UCLA. Solórzano's program at UCLA in the late 1990s is the formative environment — Lindsay Pérez Huber, Octavio Villalpando, Tara Watford, others come out of that ecology. Solórzano and Yosso publish together in 2002 — "Critical Race Methodology" in *Qualitative Inquiry* — which is the methodological companion piece to what becomes the 2005 framework. And there's a third stream you can't leave out: Luis Moll, Norma González, and Cathy Amanti's "funds of knowledge" work in anthropology of education in the early 1990s — the argument that working-class Mexican-American households contain extensive knowledge resources that schools systematically ignore.

MAYA: So why a new concept in 2005? Why not just keep writing in the funds-of-knowledge tradition or the CRT tradition? What did "community cultural wealth" do that those didn't?

DEV: Two things. First, strategic vocabulary. Yosso chose to fight Bourdieu on Bourdieu's terms. The field was using the word "capital" to describe what these students supposedly lacked. To get traction, she needed to use that word and turn it. "Funds of knowledge" was a beautiful framing but it was operating in a different vocabulary than the one being used against these communities. Second, the political moment. Think about 2005. No Child Left Behind passes in 2001, and the federal frame for K-12 — and increasingly for the higher ed pipeline conversation — is the "achievement gap," disaggregated by race. *Grutter v. Bollinger* in 2003 preserves race-conscious admissions but anchors them in diversity benefits to all students, not in any historical redress account. The DREAM Act has been introduced and defeated repeatedly. Proposition 209 in California has eliminated race-conscious admissions in the UC system, where Yosso is sitting, for nearly a decade.

MAYA: So she's writing from inside an institution that's been operating under an affirmative action ban — watching what happens to enrollment and to research conversation when the legal vocabulary for race gets restricted.

DEV: Right. And in that environment, "deficit thinking" is the dominant explanatory frame for why disaggregated achievement looks the way it does. Richard Valencia's 1997 edited volume *The Evolution of Deficit Thinking* names that pattern and traces it back through a century of educational research — but Valencia's critique didn't have a portable replacement framework. Yosso did.

[pause]

**THE CORE FRAMEWORK**

MAYA: Let's get into the six capitals. But before we list them, give me the move underneath them, because I think that's the part people skip.

DEV: The move is an inversion. The deficit question asks: what do these students lack that the dominant culture has? Yosso reframes: what wealth do these students bring that schools fail to recognize? It's not additive — she's not saying these students have cultural capital plus a few other things. She's saying the framework of "capital" itself, as the field was using it, was misnaming what was happening in the classroom. From the 2005 article: "Communities of Color are places with multiple strengths. In contrast, deficit scholars bemoan a lack of cultural capital." She's targeting a specific scholarly habit — naming names, including E. D. Hirsch and his "cultural literacy" line — and replacing the habit with a new accounting of what's already in the room.

MAYA: Okay. The six.

DEV: Aspirational capital is the ability to hold onto hopes and dreams for the future in the face of barriers, real or perceived. Yosso's example, from her own research and the Solórzano lineage, is the parent who tells the kid "you're going to college" before there's any visible mechanism for that to happen. The aspiration itself is a resource that gets transmitted intergenerationally.

MAYA: That's the one that gets misread the most, I think. Because it sounds like grit.

DEV: It can be read that way, and we'll come back to that. Linguistic capital is the intellectual and social skills gained through communication in more than one language or style. Bilingual students, kids who code-switch, kids who learned to tell stories at the kitchen table in two registers — that's a cognitive and rhetorical resource. Familial capital is the cultural knowledge nurtured among kin that carries a sense of community history, memory, and cultural intuition. It extends beyond the nuclear family — godparents, comadres and compadres, elders. Social capital is networks of people and community resources — but Yosso means something different than Bourdieu or Coleman or Putnam mean. She means the networks that get a student to college not by knowing the dean but by knowing who in the community has navigated this before.

MAYA: And then the two that I think are the most analytically distinctive.

DEV: Navigational capital — the skills of maneuvering through social institutions that were not created with you in mind. This is the student who figures out the financial aid appeal process, who learns which faculty member to ask about graduate school, who decodes the unwritten curriculum. And resistant capital — the knowledge and skills cultivated through oppositional behavior that challenges inequality. This is the one that's downstream most directly from CRT. It traces to a long Black and Chicana feminist tradition — the family teaching the child that the way the world treats them is not just; the politicized consciousness that becomes a resource for action.

MAYA: Walk me through a concrete student. Because I want to see how these don't just decompose into platitudes.

DEV: Imagine a first-generation Mexican American student — let's call her Esmeralda — arriving at a regional public university in Texas. Her grandmother lives in the household. Her parents both work, her mother in home healthcare, her father in construction. She speaks Spanish at home, English at school, a third register with her younger siblings. In high school she organized a walkout when her district tried to cut bilingual programs.

MAYA: Okay.

DEV: Deficit reading: low parental education, no one in the home with college experience, English-language learner background, behavioral incidents on the high school transcript. Six items checked off the "at-risk" inventory before she's set foot on campus. Yosso's reading: aspirational capital from a family that's been telling her since she was eight that she's going to college, even without knowing the FAFSA. Linguistic capital — she's been mediating between English-speaking institutions and Spanish-speaking elders since middle school, which is a complex rhetorical task. Familial capital — her grandmother is a knowledge repository about everything from food and medicine to who in the community to call when things go wrong. Social capital — there's a cousin who graduated from a community college two years ago and she's been on the phone with that cousin about how registration works. Navigational capital — she figured out the FAFSA herself, including the parts about a non-tax-filing parent, which the financial aid office routinely gets wrong. Resistant capital — her walkout in high school was an education in collective action, public argument, dealing with adults in power.

MAYA: Now, devil's advocate. A practitioner could hear that and say: great, that's a nice reframe, but Esmeralda still has a 2.6 GPA and is one academic warning away from losing her aid. The capitals don't pay the bills.

DEV: This is exactly the right pushback, and Yosso would say two things. First, the move is not to deny that institutions impose real consequences. The move is to refuse the explanation that the consequences track an inner deficiency of Esmeralda or her family. Second — and this is the CRT part that's often dropped — the framework is supposed to be paired with a critique of the institution. The question is not just "what does Esmeralda bring" but "what does this institution refuse to see, and what would it have to change to see it?" The 2005 article uses the phrase "to transform education and empower People of Color to utilize assets already abundant in their communities." Transform education. Not adjust Esmeralda.

MAYA: Which is the move Sam Museus picks up in 2012 and again in 2017.

DEV: Right. Museus and Uma Jayakumar's *Creating Campus Cultures* in 2012 says, in effect, fine — if the student brings wealth and the institution can't see it, the institution is the unit of analysis. And then Museus's 2017 CECE model — Culturally Engaging Campus Environments — operationalizes that into nine indicators that an institution can be measured against. From the 2012 book: "students' problems are often multifaceted, and educators who consider multiple aspects of students' lives can more effectively address their needs." Which sounds anodyne until you contrast it with the Tinto integration tradition, which had been the dominant retention frame for thirty years and which essentially asked the student to integrate into institutional culture.

MAYA: Tinto's framework — Vincent Tinto, *Leaving College*, 1987, 1993 — basically modeled departure as a function of academic and social integration into the institution. Students who didn't integrate left. Yosso's framework, applied institutionally, flips the directional assumption.

DEV: It flips the question. From "did the student integrate" to "did the institution have the cultural capacity to receive what the student brought." That's the move that takes Yosso from a critique of how the field talks to a critique of how institutions are built.

[pause]

**EVOLUTION AND REFINEMENTS**

MAYA: So between 2005 and now, the framework gets picked up almost immediately. Shaun Harper publishes the anti-deficit achievement framework in 2010 in *New Directions for Institutional Research*, focused specifically on Black male collegians. Museus and Jayakumar in 2012. Museus's CECE in 2017. Where else does it travel?

DEV: It travels into the DACA scholarship. Roberto Gonzales's *Lives in Limbo* in 2016 is not formally a CCW book but it sits in the same intellectual ecology and uses the navigational and resistant categories implicitly throughout. It travels into STEM equity research — there's a substantial line of work by people like Ebony McGee on Black engineering students that uses CCW vocabulary. It travels into community college scholarship through people like Estela Bensimon's Equity Scorecard work at USC, although Bensimon's institutional accountability frame predates Yosso and runs in parallel.

MAYA: Is the original version still usable in 2025? Or do we need a revised one?

DEV: I'd answer that in two parts. The vocabulary is fully naturalized. If you submit a dissertation proposal in higher ed today that uses Yosso, no committee is going to ask you to defend the framework's existence. That's a real shift from where we were in 2008. But the question of whether the framework as Yosso wrote it can hold up under the weight it's been asked to carry — that's open. The framework was a compact theoretical move in a journal article. It was not designed to be a full empirical research program, a survey instrument, a programmatic structure, an accreditation indicator. And as it's been pressed into each of those roles, the CRT scaffolding underneath it has thinned.

MAYA: Give me the clearest case of that.

DEV: Museus's CECE model is the clearest case, and I want to be careful here because the move is intellectually defensible and the work is rigorous. CECE operationalizes the post-Yosso turn into measurable institutional indicators — cultural familiarity, culturally relevant knowledge, cultural community service, cross-cultural engagement, and so on, with quantitative survey scales. The dependent variable in the empirical work is typically sense of belonging and persistence — graduation rates, retention.

MAYA: Which sounds like Tinto-style outcomes.

DEV: Which is exactly the tension. Tinto's outcomes survive even though Tinto's directional logic is rejected. So the radical edge of Yosso's CRT grounding — the counter-storytelling, the centrality of racism, the critique of liberalism — gets sanded down when the question becomes "does CECE predict six-year graduation." It's not that Museus is wrong to operationalize. It's that operationalization always exacts a cost, and the cost here is the loss of the part of the framework that was hardest for institutions to hear.

MAYA: So is the original version still usable, or did the reconstructions replace it?

DEV: I think the original is still usable, and arguably more useful now than the reconstructions, precisely because it has not been operationalized. The 2005 article reads, today, as a more cutting document than most of what cites it. The reconstructions are useful for institutional research. The original is useful for keeping the field honest about what the framework was supposed to do.

[pause]

**LIVE DEBATES**

MAYA: Where is the concept contested right now? Give me the substantive criticisms.

DEV: I'll give you three, from different directions, and try to give each one a fair hearing.

First criticism, from inside CRT and from settler colonial studies. The charge is that "capital" was the wrong word. Eve Tuck and K. Wayne Yang's 2012 piece "Decolonization is Not a Metaphor" — uptake in higher ed comes later, mostly post-2017 — argues that framing community resources in the language of capital concedes the economic frame Yosso was trying to escape. If everything is a kind of capital, the underlying logic of commensurability and accumulation is preserved. The student's grandmother's knowledge becomes a unit that can be tallied and, in principle, exchanged. Critics in this line argue that the framework can be assimilated into a managerial discourse about "leveraging student assets for institutional outcomes," and that this is not an accidental misuse but a vulnerability built into the original framing.

MAYA: What would Yosso's defenders say?

DEV: That the move was strategic. In 2005, the word that was being used against these communities was capital. To engage the field where it was, you had to use that word and turn it. The defender's claim is that "wealth" did rhetorical work that "knowledge" or "resources" or "competence" could not have done — it staged the inversion in the exact vocabulary the field was using. I find that defense persuasive at the level of 2005 strategy. I find it less persuasive at the level of 2025 usage, because the framework's success has detached it from the rhetorical context that motivated the original choice.

MAYA: Second criticism.

DEV: That the framework has been domesticated. This is the closest reading of citation patterns — which I want to be honest, I am not aware of a published systematic study of, so I'm reporting an impression that circulates in the field. The pattern is that Yosso is invoked, the six capitals are listed, and then the actual research design proceeds without engaging counter-storytelling, the centrality of racism, the critique of liberal colorblindness — any of the CRT epistemological commitments the framework was embedded in. The six become a checklist. The framework becomes a typology applied to interview data. And this is, ironically, exactly the move Yosso made against Bourdieu — taking a critique and turning it into a property inventory.

MAYA: Push back on me here, though. Isn't there a kind of unfalsifiability in the domestication critique? Any time a framework gets used widely, you can say "but they're not really doing the deep version." How do we tell the difference between actual domestication and ordinary scholarly uptake, where some work is shallow and some is deep?

DEV: That's fair, and I don't want to be the person who claims to know the True Yosso. I think the answer is to look at what work the framework is doing in a given paper. If a study uses CCW to argue that an institution should change its practices, that's one thing. If it uses CCW to describe what students bring without ever turning the lens on the institution, that's a different thing. The first is consistent with Yosso. The second is closer to what Bourdieu's importers did — a property inventory without the structural critique. So I'd answer your unfalsifiability worry by saying: there's a usable distinction between framework-as-critique and framework-as-description. The domestication charge is that the framework is increasingly being used in the second mode.

MAYA: Third criticism.

DEV: Method. CRT methodologies privilege counter-storytelling, testimonio, the narrative as evidence. Higher education's evidence regimes — accreditation, federal data, IPEDS, ranking systems, grant reviewers — privilege quantitative outcomes and causal inference designs. IES, the federal Institute of Education Sciences, has funded relatively little CCW-aligned work, and the reason is a methodological mismatch with IES's causal-inference preferences. So CCW scholars have faced a real bind: operationalize for legitimacy and risk losing the CRT grounding, or stay close to the methodological commitments and risk being unfunded and uncited outside CRT-specific venues. David Gillborn, Paul Warmington, and Sean Demack's 2018 QuantCrit work tries to thread this needle by translating CRT epistemology into quantitative practice. The jury is genuinely out on whether QuantCrit is a coherent methodological position or a compromise that pleases nobody.

MAYA: Where do you land?

DEV: On the method question, I land on uncomfortable pluralism. There's no version of higher ed research that operates only in counter-storytelling and gets institutional traction, and there's no version that operates only in causal inference and respects what Yosso was doing. The honest answer is that the field needs both, and the people who do both well — Lori Patton Davis, Estela Bensimon, Shaun Harper at different moments — are doing methodological work the field has not yet figured out how to credit.

MAYA: I want to push back on one more thing. You've described this as a framework that the field absorbed quickly. But "the field" in higher ed is also stratified — the *Journal of Higher Education* and the *Review of Higher Education* were slower to publish CCW work than *Race Ethnicity and Education* or the *Journal of Diversity in Higher Education*. Is the uptake story partly a story about which journals counted as "the field"?

DEV: That's right, and it's a sharper version of the question than I've heard asked. The framework's center of gravity was in *Race Ethnicity and Education*, *International Journal of Qualitative Studies in Education*, *Journal of Hispanic Higher Education* — venues with their own intellectual character. The flagship journals admitted CCW work more slowly, and tracking when they began to is a better index of mainstreaming than raw citation counts. I'd put the inflection somewhere around 2014 to 2016, though I'd want to look at this carefully before standing on a year.

**WHAT THE FIELD GETS WRONG**

DEV: One thing the field routinely flattens: aspirational capital gets read as grit. Programs adopt the language of "honoring students' aspirations" and then build interventions that look indistinguishable from Angela Duckworth's grit programming circa 2013.

MAYA: Which is the deficit framing wearing different clothes.

DEV: Right. Aspirational capital, in Yosso's framing, is not an individual psychological trait. It is an intergenerational, community-held resource that gets cultivated in families and neighborhoods in the face of structural exclusion. The aspiration is evidence of resistance to a system that has been telling these families for generations that college is not for them. To recode that as a personal trait that students should be coached to maintain when things get hard is to strip out the politics. It's also to put the burden back on the student. Grit pedagogy says: when the institution fails you, dig deeper. Yosso's framework says: when the institution fails you, the institution has failed; the aspiration was already there.

MAYA: The "actually" is: aspirational capital is not about coaching individual perseverance; it's about recognizing what families have been doing politically for generations that the institution refuses to count.

DEV: And the same flattening happens with resistant capital. It gets read as "students of color have leadership potential," which is true but trivializing. Resistant capital, in the CRT lineage, is specifically political — it's knowledge cultivated through opposition to inequality. The student who organized the walkout is not exhibiting "leadership potential." She's exhibiting a politicized consciousness that the institution would, in many cases, prefer not to receive.

[pause]

**IMPLICATIONS FOR YOUR WORK**

MAYA: Let's get concrete. If you're a doctoral student designing a study right now, what does this framework actually demand of you?

DEV: Three things. First, don't use the six capitals as interview codes. If your codebook has six categories and they're called aspirational, linguistic, familial, social, navigational, resistant — you've turned the framework into the property inventory it was designed to oppose. The categories were heuristic, not analytic units. Use them to think with, not to sort with.

MAYA: That's a hard one because dissertation committees love a clean codebook.

DEV: They do, and you may have to write a methods chapter that defends the choice not to do that. Second thing: the unit of analysis question. If your study uses CCW and the institution doesn't appear as a unit of analysis — if you are only studying students and what they bring — you are doing half the framework. The other half is what the institution refuses to see. So a CCW study that doesn't include institutional ethnography, document analysis, policy reading, or staff interviews is incomplete by the framework's own logic. Third thing: counter-storytelling. If you are working in this tradition, the methodological commitment to centering the experiential knowledge of people of color is not optional. That changes how you write interview protocols, how you handle disconfirming evidence, how you write the discussion section. Solórzano and Yosso's 2002 *Qualitative Inquiry* piece is the place to go for this.

MAYA: For student affairs practitioners — Monday morning.

DEV: Two things. One, audit your intake forms and your advising conversations for what they assume students lack. The standard "needs assessment" architecture in student affairs is built on a deficit logic. Adding a question about "strengths" does not undo the architecture. Two, when you find yourself describing a student as "at-risk," ask what the institution is at risk of. Often the honest answer is that the institution is at risk of failing this student in a way that will show up in retention data. That's a different sentence than "this student is at risk."

MAYA: For faculty teaching first-year courses.

DEV: The linguistic capital point matters a lot. Students who code-switch, who write in registers other than academic English, who think in two languages — those students are doing cognitive work that the standard rubric does not credit. That doesn't mean abandon academic writing. It means be honest about the fact that you are teaching a particular register and not a universal one, and that the students who arrive without it are not less capable; they are encountering a specific cultural form for the first time. Vershawn Ashanti Young's work on code-meshing is relevant here.

MAYA: For administrators.

DEV: Two things, and the second is harder. First, the CECE model gives you an institutional self-assessment instrument that's directly usable. If you want a way to operationalize this work without inventing your own framework, Museus's nine indicators are the cleanest path. Second, the harder thing: ask whether your DEI infrastructure, post-2020, is doing the political work of the framework or whether it has become a managerial discourse. Anti-DEI legislation in Texas, Florida, and elsewhere is going to force this question whether administrators want to face it or not. The framework can survive that political pressure. The infrastructure that carried the framework may not.

[pause]

**READING ROADMAP**

DEV: Four texts, in this order.

First, Yosso 2005 itself. Read the whole article, not the table of six capitals. The table circulates without the argument; the argument is in the prose. Pay attention to how she sets up the Bourdieu critique, because the inversion only makes sense against the specific way Bourdieu had been domesticated.

Second, Solórzano and Yosso's 2002 *Qualitative Inquiry* piece, "Critical Race Methodology." This is the methodological companion. It tells you what counter-storytelling is, what it isn't, and what evidentiary commitments come with it. Without this, the 2005 piece reads as a typology rather than as a methodological intervention.

Third, Ladson-Billings and Tate's 1995 *Teachers College Record* article. This is the upstream piece. It tells you what CRT in education was trying to do, and it makes Yosso's intervention legible as an extension of a specific tradition rather than as a freestanding contribution.

Fourth, Museus and Jayakumar's 2012 *Creating Campus Cultures* — at least the introductory and conclusion chapters. This is the institutional turn. Read it after the prior three because you'll be able to see what's preserved from Yosso, what's adapted, and where the unresolved tension with the Tinto integration tradition sits.

If you have appetite for a fifth: Eve Tuck and Wayne Yang's "Decolonization is Not a Metaphor," 2012, *Decolonization: Indigeneity, Education and Society*. Not a CCW piece, but the sharpest available critique of how asset-based frameworks can be assimilated into institutional logics that they were meant to challenge.

[pause]

**WHAT WE DON'T KNOW**

MAYA: The genuinely open question.

DEV: Whether anti-deficit scholarship can do its work in a policy environment that prohibits race-conscious institutional response. The 2023 *Students for Fair Admissions* decision ended race-conscious admissions. Anti-DEI legislation in roughly a dozen states has dismantled or restricted the administrative infrastructure that carried Yosso's vocabulary. The framework was built in an era when institutions could, at least formally, name race as a category for institutional attention. We are now in an era when many institutions formally cannot. Community cultural wealth was an intervention into what institutions and researchers say about students of color. We don't yet know what it means to do that intervention when the legal architecture that surrounded it has been removed — whether the framework becomes more important as a scholarly resource precisely because the policy levers have shrunk, or whether it loses purchase because the institutional partners it was trying to reach are no longer permitted to act on it. That's the question the next decade of scholarship has to answer, and nobody I've read has answered it well yet.
