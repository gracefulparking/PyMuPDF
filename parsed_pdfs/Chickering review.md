As I was pulling my thoughts together for
this review I was grading the second set of
final papers for the initial theory course
in our graduate program. This first-of-two
theory course is focused on constructivist-
developmental theories. I’ve taught our
second theory course, grounded in identities
and critical theories, for 22 years and I found
myself surprised at how many students used
Chickering and Reisser’s (1993) theoretical
concepts to make meaning of their own lived
experiences. I was surprised because my critical
theory focus from studying emergent identity
theories rooted in oppression and the politics
of power moved me to sideline most early
developmental theories. Ironically, my critical
epistemological focus on social inequity and
positional subjectivities overshadowed, rather
than illuminated, important commonalities in
the students’ growth experiences discussed by
Chickering in 1969, half a century ago.
In that seminal work, Chickering set out
to challenge higher education’s concentration
on information dissemination and professional
training to include a much more holistic
consideration of students’ psychosocial lives.

is used to frame hundreds of studies using
both quantitative and qualitative designs.
The wealth of published, peer-reviewed
studies demonstrates the clear utility and
applicability of the seven vectors in framing
our understanding of general developmental
tasks that college students experience. Recent
exploratory factor analysis partially vali-

confirming six out of the seven vectors with
varying levels of internal consistency. The
independence of these factors was confirmed
through correlation analysis (Midili, 2013).

### INITIAL AND ENDURING VALUE
### OF _EDUCATION AND IDENTITY_

The initial value of Chickering’s work lies
in helping shift higher education’s narrow
concentration on both cognitive-intellectual
and career-training outcomes, as if these were
disconnected from other aspects of students’ full
humanity. By outlining a conceptual framework
faithful to the broader developmental tasks
faced by students and providing research-
informed strategies for institutional action
that could effectively address student learning,
Chickering stimulated maybe more research
and influenced more institutional program and
practices than any other framework in student
affairs. Chickering and Reisser’s revision
demonstrated the enduring value of the work
by showing how the original framework could
be adapted through new findings, theoretical
considerations, and challenges that helped
nuance previous articulations. The relatively
clear and utilitarian vectors provide educators
and student affairs professionals insightful
focus on central developmental tasks that are
still quite relevant today. Educators should
continue to focus individual and programmatic
efforts toward the general vectors, once
limitations described below are integrated.
The increasingly complex question of how
to most effectively utilize institutional resources
to provide effective in-class and out-of-class
experiences that promote growth and learning
for all students will continue to challenge
higher education leaders. The institutional
factors outlined in the second half of _Education_
_and Identity_provide an initial map for shaping
environments to maximize educational impact.
In addition, the three admonitions for shaping
the interdependent institutional factors are
crucial for success: integration of work and
learning, recognition and respect for individual
differences, and remembering the cyclical
nature and disequilibrium involved in learning

### GAPS AND INSUFFICIENCIES

Like all theories, Chickering’s developmental
framework and its revision by Chickering
and Reisser reflect its historical moments
and epistemological assumptions. Research
methods used for studies considered in the
development of the theory were generally
positivist in 1969 and moved toward more
con structivist underpinnings by 1993 and later.
Positivism (knowledge is objective, predictable,
measurable) and constructivism (multiple
realities exist and knowledge is coconstructed)
can be effective epistemological perspectives
for understanding certain phenomenon;
however, when studying human identities or
systems of higher education, critical theoretical
perspectives more effectively capture aspects
of human development and institutions that
are hazy, ignored, and often go unnamed.
The constructivist assumptions, therefore,
that underlie Chickering and Reisser’s theory
leads to limitations regarding the utility of
their framework, particularly with regard to
identity development.
Chickering and Reisser stated: “In the
global society of the twenty-first century, where
change is the only certainty, not socialization
but identity formation becomes the central
and continuing task of education” (p. 208).
The centrality of identity development
makes it critical to adequately capture its
complexity. Constructivist conceptualizations
like Chickering and Reisser’s, however, fail to
adequately describe the formation of identity
within the dynamic contours of structural
inequality. Critical and poststructural schol-
ars like Butler (2004) and Jones, Abes,
and Quaye (2013) challenge constructivist

### MOVING FORWARD

The post–1993 explosion of research on sexual
orientation, trans* identities, and a whole host
of other social identities opens up a huge gap
in the theory. While research has demonstrated
that Chickering and Reisser’s theory does
not adequately account for critical cultural
differences and individual subjectivities,
it doesn’t mean that it shouldn’t be used.
Rather, it should be used in much the same
way they constructed the revised framework:
by attending to feedback, accounting for new
theoretical perspectives and research, and
honing the theory so that it more appropriately
and accurately helps educators provide indi-
vidual and programmatic interventions that
promote learning and development.

### CONCLUSION

While _Educating and Identity_ captures central
developmental tasks college students face,
has generated a wealth of scholarship and
practical applications, and focused attention
on key institutional influences that impact
learning and growth, like any theory, it should
be used with caution and within important
limitations. The challenge to closing some gaps
in the theory using a critical theory perspective
is not just philosophical. Chickering and
Reisser argue for “policies and practices
to create higher education environments
that will foster broad-based development
of human talent and potentials. We believe
that this orientation and resulting changes
will restore public confidence and political
investments” (p. xi). I would argue that there
is little evidence supporting this dream and
that we actually have regressed. Effectively
influencing public confidence and stimulating
political investments will require leaders and
educators to use strategies rooted in critical
and poststructural epistemologies to uncover
the systematic forces that keep Chickering and
Reisser’s dream from coming true.
