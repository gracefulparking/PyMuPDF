# Scholar Stacks, Episode: Leadership Identity Development

[COLD OPEN]

DEV: Picture this. It's 2003, University of Maryland College Park. A doctoral student is sitting across from an undergraduate who, by every external measure, is a leader. President of something. Runs a service org. The kind of student you'd nominate for a Truman. And the researcher asks her, "When did you start thinking of yourself as a leader?" And the student says — and I'm paraphrasing the kind of answer that shows up across these transcripts — "Oh, I'm not really a leader. I just do stuff with my friends." And then later in the same interview she describes coordinating a forty-person team, mediating conflict, building consensus across a campus coalition.

MAYA: So she's doing leadership and refusing the label.

DEV: Refusing it. And the question Susan Komives and her team start to wrestle with is — what is that gap? Because if leadership is a position, this student has it. If leadership is a set of behaviors, she's exhibiting them. But she does not have the identity. She doesn't see herself in the word. And that gap turns out to be the entire thing the field had been missing.

MAYA: That hits something I'm dealing with right now, honestly. I'm advising a peer mentor program, and half my students will tell you flat out they're not leaders. They're the most relationally skilled people in the building. And every leadership curriculum I have access to assumes the student has already accepted the identity. We start there. We start at, "Now that you're a leader…" And these students are nowhere near that opening line.

DEV: Right. And that's the puzzle the Leadership Identity Development model — LID — is built to solve. Not "how do we teach leadership skills." But "how does someone come to see themselves as a leader in the first place, and what does that even mean when they get there?"

[pause]

[INTELLECTUAL ORIGINS]

MAYA: Okay, set the table for me. Where does LID actually come from? Because I know the 2005 paper — Komives, Owen, Longerbeam, Mainella, Osteen, *Journal of College Student Development*. But the paper doesn't fall from the sky.

DEV: It absolutely doesn't. And this is one of those cases where you can't understand the theory without understanding the infrastructure that produced it. So let me back up to about 1990. The National Clearinghouse for Leadership Programs gets founded at Maryland. That same window, the U.S. Department of Education starts handing out Eisenhower Leadership Grants — federal money, seeded to dozens of campuses, to build leadership programs.

MAYA: Federal money for leadership programs feels surprising now.

DEV: It is surprising now. But this is post–Cold War, the country's having an anxiety attack about civic disengagement — Robert Putnam's "Bowling Alone" essay comes out in '95, the book in 2000 — and there's a real institutional appetite for "we need to develop the leaders of tomorrow." Some of that money flows to the Higher Education Research Institute at UCLA, where Helen Astin is leading a team — with Alexander Astin, with Komives in the working group — that produces in 1996 *A Social Change Model of Leadership Development: Guidebook Version III.*

MAYA: The Seven C's.

DEV: The Seven C's. Consciousness of self, congruence, commitment, collaboration, common purpose, controversy with civility, citizenship. That document becomes the lingua franca of the field. But — and this is the move — the Social Change Model is a values framework. It tells you what good leadership looks like. It does not tell you how a twenty-year-old gets there.

MAYA: So you have a values map, you don't have a developmental theory.

DEV: Exactly. And meanwhile, in the broader leadership literature, Joseph Rost has published *Leadership for the Twenty-First Century* in 1991, and Rost names the enemy. He calls it the "industrial paradigm" — leadership as position, as hierarchy, as the great man at the top. He says we need a "post-industrial" paradigm. Relational. Collaborative. Process-oriented. James MacGregor Burns had already split transactional from transformational leadership in '78. Margaret Wheatley is bringing in systems thinking. So there's this whole intellectual project of dethroning the great-man model.

MAYA: And LID is a child of that project.

DEV: A child of that project, yes, but also a child of constructive-developmental psychology — Robert Kegan, *In Over Our Heads* in '94 — and a child of psychosocial identity theory, the Erikson-through-Chickering line. The 2005 paper is a hybrid. It takes the post-industrial leadership values from Rost and the SCM, the stage logic from Kegan and Chickering, and the methodology from Glaser and Strauss — classic grounded theory.

MAYA: So wait, why did they need a new concept? Why couldn't they just keep using the Social Change Model and Chickering's vectors?

DEV: Because neither one answered the developmental question for leadership specifically. Chickering has a vector for "developing competence" and one for "developing purpose," but nothing for "becoming a leader." And the SCM has values but no trajectory. The field was running leadership programs — proliferating leadership programs — with no theory of how the thing they were trying to produce actually formed in a person.

MAYA: And in the meantime the political context is heating up. Spellings Commission is about to drop in 2006 demanding outcomes. *Learning Reconsidered* comes out in 2004 — student affairs needs to justify itself as education.

DEV: That's the institutional pressure. You need outcomes language. You need something measurable. The 2005 paper publishes in November-December '05. The Multi-Institutional Study of Leadership launches in 2006 under John Dugan and Komives. You can almost see the gears interlocking.

MAYA: It's a theory that arrives exactly when the field needs it to be the theory.

DEV: Which is part of why it travels so fast. Within five years it's the default framework in leadership minors, fraternity and sorority advising, NACA programming. There's a textbook — *Exploring Leadership* — that gets rebuilt around it. There's a national instrument generating data. It becomes infrastructure.

[pause]

[THE CORE FRAMEWORK]

MAYA: Okay. Walk me through what the model actually claims. Stages, mechanisms, all of it.

DEV: Six stages. And the central move, the thing you have to hold onto, is the pivot from leadership-as-position to leadership-as-identity. The paper itself says students "shifted their view of leadership to something many in a group do and as a process among people, which is more consistent with the post-industrial view of leadership."

MAYA: So that shift is what they're tracking.

DEV: That's the shift. The stages are: awareness, exploration/engagement, leader identified, leadership differentiated, generativity, and integration/synthesis. Let me ground this in a student. Let's call her Priya. First-year, comes in from a suburban high school where she was on the swim team and helped run a food drive.

Stage one — awareness. Priya as a kid notices that there are people called leaders. Coaches. Principals. The president. Leadership is something other people do. It's external to her. It's authority.

Stage two — exploration/engagement. Priya joins things. High school clubs, the swim team, residence hall council her first semester. She's involved. She's not leading anything formally. She's tasting group life.

Stage three — leader identified. This is where Priya gets elected secretary of her residence hall council. And here's the key — in this stage, she equates leadership with the position. The leader is the president. The vice president is also a leader. She, as secretary, is starting to think of herself as a leader-in-training because she has a title. And — this is from the paper — the cognition here is hierarchical. The corpus is explicit: "Hierarchical thinking was the view of leadership held in *leader identified*."

MAYA: So leadership lives in the org chart.

DEV: Leadership lives in the org chart. And critically, Priya in this stage thinks her job as a leader is to do the leadership things — make decisions, set direction. The followers follow.

Stage four — leadership differentiated. This is the big subject-object shift. Priya becomes president of a service org her sophomore year, and somewhere in there she realizes that the most important things happening in the group aren't the things she's doing from the chair. They're happening between members. They're happening when she's not in the room. And she starts to see leadership as a *process* the whole group is doing, not a thing she does to them. The corpus says systemic thinking emerges here.

MAYA: That's the post-industrial flip showing up developmentally.

DEV: Exactly. And the model claims — and this is a strong claim — that hierarchical thinking developmentally precedes systemic thinking. You can't skip stage three. You have to go through "I am the leader" before you can arrive at "leadership is what we're doing together."

MAYA: Hold on. That's the claim I want to push on. Because I've watched students — particularly students of color, particularly first-gen students — show up to college already practicing collectivist leadership. Already thinking in terms of "we" rather than "I'm in charge." Are we really saying they have to regress into hierarchical thinking before they can become leadership-differentiated?

DEV: That is the exact critique that has been leveled at this model, and we'll get to it head-on in a bit. The 2005 paper's defense would be that the stages describe how students *come to identify as leaders*, not how they practice leadership behavior. But I think you're right that the model has real trouble with students who arrive already operating in stage four logic.

MAYA: Okay, keep going. Stages five and six.

DEV: Stage five — generativity. Priya is a junior or senior now. She starts caring about the sustainability of the work beyond her tenure. She mentors younger members. She's thinking about succession. She wants what she's built to outlast her. This is, by the way, deeply Eriksonian — generativity is Erikson's mid-adult task and the model is borrowing it down into late college.

Stage six — integration/synthesis. Priya internalizes leadership as part of who she is across contexts. Not "I'm a leader because I'm president of X." But "I am the kind of person who engages relationally in groups, period — whether I have a title or not, whether the context is work or family or community." Leadership becomes part of her self-concept, integrated with her other identities.

MAYA: That last stage is doing a lot of work. The corpus actually flags this — that the late stages quietly assume Kegan's self-authorship cognition.

DEV: Which is the substrate the model doesn't entirely make explicit. The 2006 follow-up paper, the practitioner-facing one, surrounds the six stages with three developmental influences. They call them "developing self," "group influences," and "changing view of self with others." And they identify the engines that move students through — adult mentors, peer relationships, meaningful involvement, and reflective learning.

MAYA: Mentors do a lot of work in this model.

DEV: They do. And the corpus is explicit about practice: "Developmental interventions targeted at expanding identity development are indicated in the LID model and support much of the current educational and developmental strategies used on college campuses."

MAYA: Which is why advisors love this model. It tells them their job is real. The advisor is a developmental mechanism.

DEV: The advisor is in the theory. That's part of why it traveled.

[pause]

[EVOLUTION AND REFINEMENTS]

MAYA: So how has this changed since 2005? Because it's almost twenty years old now.

DEV: The honest answer is — and this is from the lineage map I want to be straight about — LID has been *extended* a lot and *reconstructed* very little. The 2006 paper is the practitioner translation. *Exploring Leadership*, third edition in 2013, rebuilds the textbook around LID. The Multi-Institutional Study of Leadership becomes the empirical engine — by mid-decade it's got over 250,000 student responses across hundreds of institutions.

MAYA: That's a massive dataset.

DEV: Massive. And the MSL produces a continuous stream of papers — leadership efficacy, socially responsible leadership, the impact of mentorship, race-comparative findings. But here's what I'd push you to notice. The MSL is mostly testing predictors of leadership outcomes. It's not interrogating whether the six-stage developmental sequence holds up across populations in a way that would force the model to change.

MAYA: So the empirical work confirms the framework rather than stress-testing it.

DEV: Largely, yes. The model gets applied. It does not get rebuilt. Compare this to what happened to the Model of Multiple Dimensions of Identity — Jones and McEwen 2000. Five years later, Elisa Abes, Susan Jones, and Marylu McEwen come back and reconceptualize it. They add a meaning-making filter. The corpus describes this — they bring in Baxter Magolda's self-authorship and Kegan's orders of consciousness to explain *why* contextual influences are differentially powerful for different students.

MAYA: They actually rebuilt the model.

DEV: They rebuilt the model. The MMDI of 2007 is a different object than the MMDI of 2000. LID has not had that moment. There's no Komives 2012 paper saying, "Here is what we now think the stages are, given the critiques."

MAYA: Why not, do you think?

DEV: Part of it is that the model became infrastructure too quickly. There are leadership minors built on the six stages. There are AFA — Association of Fraternity/Sorority Advisors — curricula built on it. There's a textbook. Changing the model means changing all of that.

MAYA: There's a path-dependency problem.

DEV: There's a path-dependency problem. The other piece is that critical reconstruction has come from outside the original team. John Dugan — who is Komives-trained, the MSL architect — publishes *Leadership Theory: Cultivating Critical Perspectives* in 2017, and that book is, in part, an internal critique. It takes critical race theory, critical leadership studies, queer theory seriously, and asks what they do to the field's assumptions. But it doesn't really propose a successor model to LID. It proposes a stance.

MAYA: So is the original version still usable?

DEV: I think yes, but with much more humility than its 1.0 use. The original sample was thirteen students at one institution, nominated by faculty and staff as exhibiting relational leadership. That's a tiny, purposively selected, highly involved sample. The model that emerged is a really useful heuristic for thinking about how identity-as-leader forms in students who are on a particular kind of organizational trajectory. It is not — and I don't think it was ever claimed to be, originally — a universal sequence for all students.

MAYA: But that's how it's used.

DEV: That's exactly how it gets used. The corpus is honest about this — the original authors write, "It must also be recognized that for this study we examined the identity development process for students who were selected" — and then the practitioner field absorbs the model as though that caveat doesn't exist.

[pause]

[LIVE DEBATES]

MAYA: Okay, where is this contested right now? Where are the real fights?

DEV: Three fights, I'd say. Let me take them seriously one at a time.

Fight one — universality. Is this developmental sequence general, or is it specific to a population that was middle-class, mostly white, residentially involved, organizationally embedded? Tara Yosso's 2005 piece, "Whose Culture Has Capital?" — Critical Race Theory and LatCrit — argues that students from communities of color arrive with aspirational, navigational, social, familial, linguistic, and resistant capital that mainstream models render invisible. Apply that to LID and the question becomes: when a Latina first-gen student arrives on campus already practicing leadership inside her extended family — translating documents, mediating between her parents and institutions, organizing siblings — what stage is she in?

MAYA: She's not in stage one. She's been doing leadership her whole life.

DEV: She's been doing leadership her whole life. And by the model's own logic, the relational, process-oriented, collectivist orientation she brings is what the model codes as stage four. So either she's developmentally precocious — which is condescending and also wrong — or the model is reading her culture as development.

MAYA: That's a strong charge. What would a defender say?

DEV: A defender would say two things. One — the model is about how a student comes to use the *language* of leader and to claim that identity in an organizational context, not about leadership practice. Two — empirically, the MSL finds the stages show up across racial groups. The aggregate confirms.

MAYA: But the aggregate is built on a survey instrument that was constructed from the model. There's a circularity there.

DEV: There's circularity. And the deeper critique — this is from critical leadership studies, David Collinson, Dennis Tourish — is that the post-industrial frame still smuggles in psychological individualism. It's relational, but the unit of analysis is still the individual leader's identity formation. It's not actually a structural or political theory of leadership. It's a developmental psychology of the self that happens to use relational vocabulary.

MAYA: Okay. Fight two?

DEV: Fight two — activism. Post-2014, post-Mizzou, post-the Yale protests, post-Black Lives Matter on campus — the field has had to reckon with the fact that some of the most consequential student leadership on American campuses in the last decade was done by students who explicitly refused the "leader" label. Decentered organizing. Leaderful, not leader-led. adrienne maree brown's *Emergent Strategy* in 2017 becomes a touchstone.

MAYA: And LID can't really account for that, because integration/synthesis assumes you arrive at an integrated leader-identity.

DEV: Right. The telos is owning the identity. The activist's principled refusal to own it looks, in the model, like she's stuck at stage two.

MAYA: Which is insulting to a twenty-year-old organizer running circles around a leadership educator.

DEV: It's insulting and also empirically wrong. A defender of LID would say: the activist is doing leadership-differentiated work even if she's refusing the word. The model accounts for her. But the issue is more fundamental — the model treats the word, the identity claim, as the developmental endpoint. If you don't want the word, the model has no language for what you've achieved.

MAYA: And fight three?

DEV: Fight three is more methodological. It's the QuantCrit critique — Gillborn, Warmington, Demack in 2018 — applied to the MSL. The MSL is the largest dataset in the field. It's also a survey instrument administered to disproportionately residential, four-year-institution students who self-select into responding. Whose leadership development are we capturing? Not community college students. Not part-time students. Not students whose leadership happens in their churches and labor organizations and mutual aid networks.

MAYA: Push me on this one, though. Is your position that LID should be retired?

DEV: No, my position is that LID is a useful theory of a specific phenomenon — how organizationally embedded undergraduates at residential four-year institutions come to claim the leader identity — that has been overgeneralized into a theory of leadership development as such. And I think that overgeneralization is on the field, not on the original authors.

MAYA: I'm going to push back on that, because I think you're letting the original team off the hook. The 2006 paper is titled "A Leadership Identity Development Model." Not "A Model of Leadership Identity Development in Highly Involved Residential Undergraduates." The universalizing move is in the title.

DEV: That's fair. The framing of the model invites the universalization. The caveats are in the methods section. The title and the figures travel.

MAYA: Right. And once it's in a textbook, the caveats vanish.

DEV: They vanish.

[pause]

[WHAT THE FIELD GETS WRONG]

DEV: Here's the thing practitioners routinely flatten. They treat the stages as a ladder a student climbs through programming. As though if you put a sophomore through enough leadership retreats, she will progress from leader identified to leadership differentiated.

MAYA: Like it's a curriculum.

DEV: Like it's a curriculum. But the original paper is explicit — the developmental influences are mentors, peers, meaningful involvement, *and reflective learning*. The mechanism isn't exposure to programming. The mechanism is the subject-object shift in how the student is making meaning of her experience. You can stack programs all day. If the student isn't moving from "leadership is what the president does" to "leadership is something we're doing together" — if that cognitive reorganization isn't happening — she's not progressing. She's just got a longer résumé.

MAYA: So the field treats LID as a programming logic when it's actually a meaning-making logic.

DEV: That's the flattening. And the second thing — the field treats the six stages as discrete steps. The original grounded theory describes transitions as much as stages. The interesting work is in the transition zones. A student is partially in leader identified, partially seeing the systemic view. That mess is where development is actually happening.

MAYA: The model gets cleaned up for the PowerPoint slide.

DEV: It gets cleaned up for the PowerPoint slide, and then the cleaned-up version is what gets taught.

[pause]

[IMPLICATIONS FOR YOUR WORK]

MAYA: Okay, let me bring this home. If you're a doctoral student, if you're a practitioner, if you're a faculty member designing a leadership minor — what does this episode mean for Monday morning?

If you're writing a dissertation on leadership development in any population that wasn't represented in that original Maryland sample — community college students, students of color, adult learners, online students, students whose involvement is outside formal organizations — do not just apply LID and look for the stages. Test whether the stages even describe the phenomenon for your population. Use it as a frame to push against, not as a frame to confirm.

DEV: And if you're using the MSL data, be explicit about whose leadership it captures and whose it doesn't. That's not a limitations-section throwaway. That's central to the claims you can make.

MAYA: If you're an advisor — Greek life, student org, residence life — the implication is that the conversations matter more than the programming. The model says reflective learning is a developmental engine. Which means a thirty-minute one-on-one where you actually ask a student how she thinks about her role in the group is probably worth more developmentally than a Saturday leadership retreat.

DEV: And the corollary — for the student who's refusing the leader label while doing the work, don't try to push her into the identity. Ask her what she's doing and how she understands it. The point isn't to get her to say the word.

MAYA: If you're designing a leadership minor or curriculum, the question is whether your sequence is set up to actually scaffold the subject-object shift between hierarchical and systemic thinking. Most curricula I've seen front-load skills — communication, conflict, decision-making — without ever inviting students to interrogate what they think leadership *is*. That's stage-three pedagogy dressed up as stage-four content.

DEV: And if you're an administrator under anti-DEI pressure — and this is the live institutional question right now in Florida, Texas, elsewhere — the LID-influenced curriculum that absorbed identity-conscious frameworks is now politically vulnerable. The honest answer is: the universalist version of LID is the one that's politically defensible right now, and that's exactly the version that critical scholars have been arguing for twenty years is empirically thin.

MAYA: That's an uncomfortable place to be.

DEV: It's an uncomfortable place to be, and the field is in it.

[pause]

[READING ROADMAP]

DEV: Four texts, in order.

One — *A Social Change Model of Leadership Development: Guidebook Version III*, HERI 1996. Read this first because LID is unintelligible without the Seven C's and the values framework it's developmentally extending. You need the parent document.

Two — Komives, Owen, Longerbeam, Mainella, Osteen, "Developing a Leadership Identity: A Grounded Theory," *Journal of College Student Development*, 2005. Read the actual paper, not summaries of it. Pay attention to the methods section — thirteen students, one institution, purposive sampling — because that's the empirical base of everything that came after.

Three — Komives, Lucas, and McMahon, *Exploring Leadership*, third edition, 2013. This is where you see what the model looks like when it's been turned into pedagogy. The Relational Leadership Model is in here too. This is the form most of your students and most practitioners encounter the work in.

Four — John Dugan, *Leadership Theory: Cultivating Critical Perspectives*, 2017. Read this last because it's the inside-the-tradition critique. Dugan was trained by Komives. He built the MSL. And he's the one writing the book that says, here's what critical race theory, queer theory, and critical leadership studies do to our assumptions. You need to encounter the original framework before you encounter its most rigorous internal critic.

If you have time for a fifth — Tara Yosso, "Whose Culture Has Capital?" 2005. Same year as the LID paper. Read them side by side and notice what each one is and isn't seeing.

[pause]

[WHAT WE DON'T KNOW]

MAYA: One open question.

DEV: Here's mine. We don't actually know what happens to leadership identity development when the residential, peer-interaction-rich, organizationally embedded environment that LID assumed as its developmental context — when that environment doesn't exist. The pandemic was the natural experiment, and the field hasn't really metabolized it. What does leadership identity formation look like for a student whose primary social environment is a Discord server and an asynchronous class? What does it look like for a student commuting between three jobs and a community college? The model was built on a particular ecology of student life that is shrinking demographically and was suspended for two years institutionally. We do not have a theory of leadership identity development for the actual majority of American undergraduates in 2025. We have a theory for a population that is becoming a minority of the sector, and we are still teaching that theory as though it describes the whole.
