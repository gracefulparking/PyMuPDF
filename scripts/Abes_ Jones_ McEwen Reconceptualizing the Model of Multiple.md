DEV: The parent work — the work this article is explicitly reconceptualizing — is Susan Jones and Marylu McEwen's 2000 model. The Model of Multiple Dimensions of Identity. MMDI. Picture a core sense of self in the middle, and then intersecting rings around it — race, gender, sexual orientation, religion, class, culture. The rings move closer or farther from the core depending on what's salient in a given context. If you're the only Black student in a seminar, race rings closer to the core that hour. If you're at a family wedding, religion or culture might.

MAYA: And Jones and McEwen built that model from a grounded theory study of women college students in the late 90s. It was a real corrective to the field, because most identity work at that point was single-dimension — racial identity here, gender identity there, sexual identity over there, as if students experienced them one at a time.

DEV: Right. So the 2000 model captured the *what* and the *when* of multiple identities. What it didn't capture was the *how*. How does a student actually do the cognitive work of deciding what's salient? What's the internal machinery?

MAYA: And that's where Kegan and Baxter Magolda come in.

DEV: That's where they come in. Robert Kegan's constructivist-developmental theory — the orders of consciousness work — gives you a vocabulary for how people make meaning at different levels of cognitive complexity. Marcia Baxter Magolda takes that and applies it to college students specifically through her self-authorship framework. So Abes, Jones, and McEwen say: let's bolt the cognitive piece onto the multiple-identities piece. Let's put a meaning-making filter between context and identity perception. That gives you the Reconceptualized MMDI. RMMDI.

MAYA: Wait, before you move on. How is this different from what Baxter Magolda was already doing? Because self-authorship already has an interpersonal dimension, an intrapersonal dimension, and a cognitive dimension. She's already saying these talk to each other.

DEV: That's the right question. Baxter Magolda's three dimensions are domains of development that mature roughly together. What this article is doing is more specific. It's saying: when a student is perceiving the relationships among their *social* identities — not just developing an interpersonal capacity in the abstract, but actually asking "how does my being lesbian relate to my being Catholic relate to my being working-class" — meaning-making complexity is the filter that determines what answer is even available to them. Self-authorship is the engine; the RMMDI is showing what the engine does to the specific problem of intersectional identity.

MAYA: Let's get the concepts on the table. Because we've already thrown around "orders of consciousness" and "self-authorship" and "intersectionality" like everyone has them memorized.

DEV: Fair. Kegan's orders of consciousness — five increasingly complex structures for organizing experience. For college students, the relevant ones are third order, the transition between third and fourth, and fourth order. Baxter Magolda renames these in more accessible ways. Third order is what she calls *formulaic* meaning making. You define yourself by your relationships and by external formulas. "I'm a good daughter, so I do what my parents expect. I'm a Christian, so I believe what my pastor says."

MAYA: The external sources aren't questioned. They're constitutive.

DEV: Right. You can't yet step back from them and ask "do I agree?" because there's no "I" outside them yet. Then there's the *crossroads*, the transitional space. You start hearing your own voice but the external voices are still loud and you're caught between them. And then *foundational* meaning making, or self-authorship — Baxter Magolda's term, fourth order in Kegan — where you can construct your identity from internally generated values, hold relationships without losing yourself, hold knowledge contextually.

MAYA: So what's contested here. If a critical race scholar or a queer theorist read this article, what do they say?

DEV: They say several things. First, Kegan's developmental hierarchy is built on a Western, individualist notion of selfhood. MAYA: A student from a collectivist culture who continues to define herself substantially through family obligation isn't formulaic. She's operating in a different framework where the self-in-relation isn't a developmental waystation, it's the destination.
