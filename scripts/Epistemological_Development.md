# Scholar Stacks — Epistemological Development

[pause]

**DEV:** A first-year asks her chemistry TA which textbook is right, because two of her assigned readings disagree about reaction kinetics. The TA says, "Well, they're both right within their assumptions." She comes back the next week furious. She tells the TA she's paying tuition to learn what's true, not to be told that truth depends. By the end of the semester she has stopped asking questions in section. She tells her advisor she's thinking about switching majors because chemistry has "gone soft." Nothing about her intelligence has changed between September and December. Something about how she expects knowledge to behave has collided with how her professors think knowledge actually behaves. That collision — and the question of whether it resolves, and how, and over what timeline — is the territory of epistemological development.

**MAYA:** And I want to flag for listeners why this matters for me, sitting here as a doc student. Half my dissertation interviews this semester have had some version of this student in them. I have a participant who told me her favorite professor "ruined" a class by refusing to lecture. I have another who described group discussions as the professor "not doing his job." I keep coding these as resistance, or as critiques of pedagogy. But Dev, what you're describing suggests I might be coding the symptom and missing the structure.

**DEV:** Right. And the bet that this whole line of research makes is that the structure is developmental — that there's a logic to how it changes, that the change is partial and uneven, and that college, if it does anything, does this.

[pause]

## Intellectual Origins

**DEV:** So let's locate this. William Perry directed the Bureau of Study Counsel at Harvard from 1947 to 1976. The Bureau was an odd institution — a counseling-and-tutoring center with a research mandate, which meant Perry had years of clinical conversations with undergraduates about their academic difficulties, and the institutional cover to turn those conversations into a research program. Between 1954 and 1963 he and his colleagues interviewed Harvard undergraduates, mostly at the end of each academic year, asking them open-ended questions about their college experience. The 1970 book — *Forms of Intellectual and Ethical Development in the College Years* — is what came out of that.

**MAYA:** Wait, why did Perry need a new concept here? Piaget existed. Erikson existed. What gap was he filling?

**DEV:** Three things were happening at once. Piaget had given American psychology the structural-stage idea — that thinking reorganizes qualitatively, not just quantitatively — but Piaget stopped at formal operations, roughly adolescence. The question of whether anything kept developing after that was open. Lawrence Kohlberg, also at Harvard, had extended structural-stage logic into moral reasoning and had shown you could do it with longitudinal interviews. So Perry had a method and a permission. What he didn't have was a theory of how people develop in their relationship to *knowledge itself* — not what they know, but what they think knowing is. That's the gap.

**MAYA:** And the third thing?

**DEV:** Thomas Kuhn. *Structure of Scientific Revolutions* came out in 1962, expanded in 1970, same year as Perry's book. Kuhn made it intellectually live to think of knowledge as paradigm-bound rather than cumulative. Perry's students were arriving at college expecting cumulative truth and being handed paradigm conflicts. The historical moment made the developmental question urgent.

**MAYA:** Okay. What did Perry claim?

**DEV:** Nine positions, which everyone collapses into roughly four. Dualism: knowledge is right or wrong, authorities have it, your job is to receive it. Multiplicity: authorities disagree, so on contested questions everyone's opinion is equally valid. Relativism: not all opinions are equally valid — some are better supported, some are better reasoned, but knowledge is constructed within frameworks, not handed down. Commitment in relativism: you take a position, you act, you live a life, knowing your commitments are made under conditions of irreducible uncertainty.

**MAYA:** And the move from each position to the next isn't about learning more facts.

**DEV:** It's about reorganizing what facts are. That's the Piagetian inheritance.

**MAYA:** The thing I keep getting stuck on is the sample. Harvard men, 1954 to 1963. That's the silent generation, prep school pipeline, a sliver of the country.

**DEV:** Perry was explicit about this. He didn't hide it. But the field took fifteen years to metabolize what it meant. By 1979 women were over fifty percent of U.S. undergraduate enrollment — meaning Perry's sample was already an artifact of a vanishing demographic by the time his scheme became canonical in student affairs. That lag matters. It set up the Belenky critique.

**MAYA:** Which is where Mary Belenky and her co-authors come in.

**DEV:** Belenky, Blythe Clinchy, Nancy Goldberger, and Jill Tarule, *Women's Ways of Knowing*, 1986. The provocation was Carol Gilligan's 1982 *In a Different Voice*, which had done to Kohlberg what Belenky and her co-authors were about to do to Perry. Gilligan argued Kohlberg's all-male sample had produced a hierarchy that mis-scored women's moral reasoning. Belenky and colleagues conducted 135 interviews — and this is methodologically important — not just with college students. They deliberately included women in social service agencies, women outside higher education entirely. Their categories were silence, received knowing, subjective knowing, procedural knowing, which split into separate and connected, and constructed knowing.

**MAYA:** Silence isn't in Perry at all.

**DEV:** No. And it can't be, because Perry's sample was at Harvard. Nobody at Harvard in 1958 was epistemologically silenced in the way Belenky's non-college women were. The category itself is an artifact of who you talk to. That's a methodological lesson the field absorbed quickly. The deeper claim — that connected knowing, knowing through identification and empathy, is a *different mode* rather than a *lower position* — that claim got partially domesticated. We'll come back to that.

**MAYA:** And King and Kitchener are the third leg of this stool.

**DEV:** Patricia King's dissertation was 1977; her advisor was Karen Strohm Kitchener at Minnesota. They built the Reflective Judgment Model — seven stages, anchored explicitly in John Dewey's 1910 *How We Think*. Dewey defined reflective judgment as suspending belief while evidence is weighed under conditions of genuine uncertainty. King and Kitchener took that almost word for word and built a standardized interview protocol around ill-structured problems — questions like "How did the pyramids get built?" or "Are news reports trustworthy?" — where there's no algorithmic answer. They wanted tighter psychometrics than Perry had. Longitudinal data into adulthood. By the time *Developing Reflective Judgment* comes out in 1994, they have two decades of work showing this trajectory continues well past college.

## The Core Framework

**MAYA:** Let me push on what the framework actually claims. I'm going to bring back our chemistry student. Where is she at the start of the semester?

**DEV:** On a Perry reading, she's in dualism or just edging into early multiplicity. Knowledge is something authorities possess; her job is to acquire it. When two authorities — two textbook authors — disagree, that's not a paradigm conflict, it's an error somewhere that the TA should fix. When the TA says "both are right within their assumptions," she hears the TA refusing to do the TA's job.

**MAYA:** And on a Baxter Magolda reading?

**DEV:** Baxter Magolda's longitudinal Miami University study started in 1986 with 101 students — fifty-one women, fifty men — and she's followed many of them into midlife. Her terminology is absolute knowing, transitional knowing, independent knowing, contextual knowing. Listen to how she describes it in *Making Their Own Way*: the college-phase model "described the movement from absolute knowing, in which knowledge is assumed to be certain, through transitional knowing, in which some knowledge is believed to be uncertain, to independent knowing, in which knowledge is assumed to be largely uncertain. Although some evidence of contextual knowing, in which knowledge claims are made based upon relevant evidence within a context, emerged, this occurred rarely."

**MAYA:** Rarely. That's worth sitting with. Contextual knowing — which is the developmental destination during college on her model — is uncommon among graduating seniors.

**DEV:** Right. This is one of the field's quietest findings and one of its most consequential. The capacity we say we're trying to produce in undergraduate education — to weigh evidence within frameworks, hold multiple perspectives, commit anyway — is *rare* at graduation. Most students leave somewhere in independent knowing, which Baxter Magolda's later work describes as everyone-has-their-own-truth multiplicity dressed up in the language of critical thinking.

**MAYA:** That's the everything-is-an-opinion student.

**DEV:** Yes. And here's where the framework gets interesting, because Baxter Magolda follows these students past graduation. Her 2009 chapter has this passage about two participants, Chris and Ann, talking past each other. Chris views science as "tentative facts, subject to revision." Ann views the nature of science as "hard core facts." Baxter Magolda writes that "the disconnect between Chris and Ann stems from the frameworks each uses to understand learning and knowledge." Same conversation, same evidence, two epistemologies. Ann interprets Chris's examples as attempts to *prove* something to her, when Chris is portraying cryobiology as an evolving field. They cannot understand each other because the prior question — what does it mean for something to be known — has not been mutually established.

**MAYA:** So the framework's claim is that this isn't a communication problem. It's a structural mismatch.

**DEV:** And the only thing that resolves it is one of them reorganizing what knowing is. That's slow. That's not a semester.

**MAYA:** Let's name the dimensions of self-authorship, because by 2001 Baxter Magolda is no longer treating epistemological development as standalone.

**DEV:** Three dimensions. Epistemological: how do I know? Intrapersonal: who am I? Interpersonal: how do I want to be in relationships? She's explicit that her longitudinal study began with the epistemological question and that the other two dimensions surfaced because participants couldn't keep talking about how they knew without talking about who they were and who they were with. Quote: "It seemed that they could not address the epistemological dimension, or the 'how you know' question, without working with the other two dimensions."

**MAYA:** That's a fairly major theoretical concession. The original Perry program isolated epistemology. Baxter Magolda is saying you can't actually isolate it past college.

**DEV:** Right. And this is where Robert Kegan enters as the philosophical scaffolding. Kegan's orders of consciousness — particularly the move from the third order, which he calls the socialized mind, to the fourth, which he calls the self-authoring mind — gives Baxter Magolda the architecture to integrate three dimensions into a single developmental construct. Self-authorship is the internal capacity to coordinate one's own beliefs, identity, and relationships rather than having them coordinated for you by external authority.

**MAYA:** Okay, but practically — what does this look like? Take a sophomore RA dealing with a roommate conflict. Their two residents disagree about whether quiet hours have been violated. What does the framework predict?

**DEV:** An absolute-knowing RA looks for the rule. Quiet hours start at ten. Was it after ten? Case closed. A transitional-knowing RA recognizes that the rule has interpretive slack but still locates authority somewhere external — the RA handbook, the hall director, residence life policy. Independent-knowing RA says everyone has their own perspective, both of you are valid, let's just respect each other's truth, which is useless to the residents who actually have to share a wall. Contextual-knowing RA can hold that the policy exists, that interpretations differ, that this particular roommate pair has particular histories and needs, and can construct a resolution defensible within those constraints. That last RA is rare, and we expect them all to function at that level by November of sophomore year.

**MAYA:** And we get furious when they can't.

**DEV:** Right. The framework should make us less furious and more strategic.

## Evolution and Refinements

**MAYA:** Let's track the refinements. We've got Perry 1970, Belenky and colleagues 1986, King and Kitchener building through the eighties and consolidating in 1994, Baxter Magolda building through the nineties and consolidating in 2001 and 2009. What changes across that arc?

**DEV:** Four moves. First, sample expansion. Perry was Harvard men. Belenky and colleagues deliberately diversified by class and institutional setting. Baxter Magolda's sample was gender-balanced from the start and she initially reported gender-related patterns explicitly. By 2001 those patterns have receded into a unified self-authorship construct, which is itself a debatable move.

**MAYA:** Hold on. Receded how?

**DEV:** Belenky and colleagues argued separate knowing and connected knowing were *different modes*, distributed differently across gender but not hierarchically ordered. Baxter Magolda's early work in *Knowing and Reasoning in College* in 1992 preserved gendered patterns — relational and impersonal patterns within each phase. By *Making Their Own Way* in 2001, those gendered patterns have been folded into the developmental trajectory rather than running alongside it. The critique that there might be *different ways* of knowing rather than better and worse positions on one ladder — that critique did not survive the integration.

**MAYA:** That's a real loss.

**DEV:** It is. And it's the kind of loss that happens when a field consolidates around a single integrative construct under pressure from assessment movements that want measurable outcomes.

**MAYA:** Second move?

**DEV:** Methodological tightening. Perry's interviews were semi-clinical, very open. King and Kitchener built the Reflective Judgment Interview as a standardized protocol with trained scorers and inter-rater reliability statistics. This made the work more defensible in the psychometric tradition but also narrowed it. You can't get at silenced knowing through a standardized protocol about ill-structured problems.

**MAYA:** Third move?

**DEV:** Longitudinal extension. Perry stopped at graduation. King and Kitchener and Baxter Magolda all followed participants into adulthood. What they found is that contextual knowing or reflective judgment at the higher stages mostly develops *after* college, often in the late twenties or thirties, triggered by life events — career disappointments, parenting, the death of a parent, a serious relationship rupture — that demand a reorganization the college environment hadn't required.

**MAYA:** So is the original Perry version still usable?

**DEV:** As a heuristic, yes. As an empirical model, the longitudinal data has superseded it. Nobody runs new Perry-scheme studies. People run self-authorship studies and reflective judgment studies. But Perry's vocabulary — dualism, multiplicity, relativism, commitment — is still the field's pedagogical shorthand, and I'd argue that's because the nine-position scheme is genuinely clearer to undergraduates and faculty than the more sophisticated successors.

**MAYA:** Fourth move?

**DEV:** Integration with identity. Abes, Jones, and McEwen's 2007 reconceptualization of the Model of Multiple Dimensions of Identity inserts what they call a "meaning-making filter" between context and identity perception. The filter is essentially self-authorship capacity. Students with more complex meaning-making can resist contextual pressures on their identity; students with what Abes and colleagues call formulaic meaning-making absorb those pressures uncritically. That's epistemological development doing work inside identity theory.

**MAYA:** Three generations of advisor lineage in that paper, right?

**DEV:** Marylu McEwen advised Susan Jones at Maryland; Jones advised Elisa Abes at Ohio State. The 2007 paper is McEwen, Jones, Abes — actually published as Abes, Jones, McEwen — three generations writing together. Worth knowing because the intellectual continuity is also a relational continuity.

## Live Debates

**MAYA:** Where is this concept contested right now?

**DEV:** Two substantive criticisms from different directions. The first is the cultural-universality challenge. The charge, roughly: self-authorship as the developmental destination — an internal voice that coordinates beliefs, identity, and relationships — is a culturally specific telos. It privileges autonomy over relationality, the individual over the community, the internal over the communal. Tuck and Yang's *Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor* in 2012, work by Stephanie Waterman on Indigenous student experience, Leigh Patel's *Decolonizing Educational Research* in 2015 — none of them targets self-authorship directly, but cumulatively they make the case that developmental hierarchies built on Western philosophical assumptions cannot be straightforwardly extended to students from communal epistemic traditions.

**MAYA:** What's the defender's response?

**DEV:** Two moves. One is that self-authorship is not anti-relational; Baxter Magolda's third dimension is interpersonal, and the mature self-authored person, on her account, has *more* capacity for genuine relationship, not less, because they're not defending an unexamined self. The second is that the longitudinal data is what it is — participants describe experiences of reorganization that match the construct. The critique would have to show either that the construct mis-describes those participants' experiences, or that other samples would generate a different construct entirely. The decolonial critics tend to make the second argument philosophically rather than empirically.

**MAYA:** Push back here, though. That defense lets the construct off the hook. Saying "interpersonal is one of the three dimensions" doesn't address the worry that the whole architecture privileges the *internal* coordinator. If your tradition holds that wisdom is held collectively or inherited from elders or revealed rather than constructed, then the destination "I generate my own internal foundation" doesn't sound mature. It sounds atomized.

**DEV:** That's fair. And I don't think Baxter Magolda has answered it. She acknowledges it in passing. There's a 2014 chapter where she discusses the cultural specificity worry, and her response is essentially that participants from non-dominant cultural backgrounds in her sample also describe moves toward self-authorship, just with different content. The skeptic would say: of course they do, because they're being educated in institutions whose entire pedagogical architecture rewards self-authorship.

**MAYA:** What's the second criticism?

**DEV:** The structural-versus-cognitive challenge. Elisa Abes's 2009 piece "Theoretical Borderlands" and work by Lori Patton Davis, Kristen Renn, and others argues that the cognitive-structural tradition treats epistemological development as a property of individuals, when in fact what looks like developmental position is often a response to structural conditions. A first-generation student who defers to professors isn't necessarily in dualism; she may have correctly read that the institution punishes her if she challenges authority. A queer student who holds multiple incommensurable accounts of her own identity isn't necessarily in multiplicity; she's surviving incompatible normative demands. The framework can't tell the difference, and when it scores those students it tends to score them low.

**MAYA:** That's a serious charge. The framework as a mis-measurement device for marginalized students.

**DEV:** It is. And it dovetails with Tara Yosso's 2005 community cultural wealth piece, which doesn't target epistemological development directly but charges the broader student development tradition with measuring students of color against a white middle-class developmental yardstick and calling the gap deficit. If you accept Yosso's frame, the developmental hierarchy is itself a deficit instrument.

**MAYA:** Where do you land?

**DEV:** I think the framework is doing two things that can be separated. It's describing a real psychological phenomenon — the reorganization of how one holds knowledge claims — and it's also encoding a normative preference for one particular endpoint of that reorganization. The descriptive work is robust; the longitudinal data is real, and other researchers using different methods find similar patterns. The normative work is the part that needs scrutiny. We can use the framework to *understand* what's happening with our chemistry student without committing to the claim that her destination should be Baxter Magolda's contextual knower.

**MAYA:** I'm not sure you can separate them as cleanly as you're suggesting. The stages aren't named neutrally. "Absolute" sounds bad. "Contextual" sounds good. The descriptive work *is* the normative work.

**DEV:** That's a fair pushback. And I think the honest answer is that the field has not figured out how to do structural-stage developmental theory without baked-in hierarchy. That's the open question Abes is pressing on.

[pause]

## What the Field Gets Wrong

**MAYA:** Give me the one thing practitioners routinely flatten.

**DEV:** Practitioners treat epistemological position as a *trait* rather than a *structure*. You hear it constantly: "this student is in dualism" or "my freshmen are all multiplists." That language treats the position as something the student *is*, like an introvert or an extravert. Perry's whole point — and Baxter Magolda's, and King and Kitchener's — is that what's developing is a *capacity* that's domain-variable and context-dependent. The same student can reason in contextual knowing about her major and in absolute knowing about her religion and in multiplicity about politics, in the same week.

**MAYA:** Because the structure isn't a personality.

**DEV:** It's a capacity that's recruited unevenly. King and Kitchener are very clear on this. They report what they call developmental range — students perform across a band of stages, not at a single point. The modal stage tells you something, but it doesn't tell you what they're capable of under support, and it doesn't tell you what they default to under threat.

**MAYA:** What does the trait-misreading produce in practice?

**DEV:** It produces giving up. A faculty member decides her sophomores are dualists and therefore can't handle paradigm conflict, so she teaches to the modal stage. That's exactly the move the framework predicts will *prevent* development. The whole point of Baxter Magolda's Learning Partnerships Model is to validate students' capacity to know, situate learning in their experience, and define learning as mutually constructing meaning — three principles paired with three challenges. Quote from the 2009 chapter: "Participants reported greater willingness to take responsibility for constructing knowledge and their own beliefs when educators validated their potential to do so." You have to address them above their default, not at it.

**MAYA:** Vygotsky's zone of proximal development, basically.

**DEV:** Functionally yes. And the trait reading inverts it.

## Implications for Your Work

**MAYA:** Let me try to make this concrete. If you're a doc student studying first-generation student experience and you're coding interviews — what does this concept change about your method?

**DEV:** It changes what you do with disagreement-talk. When a participant says her professor "wasn't doing his job" because he wouldn't lecture, the temptation is to code that as a critique of pedagogy and move on. The framework suggests a second pass: what is this participant's working theory of what knowledge is and where it comes from? You're not diagnosing her — you're treating her epistemological assumptions as data about how she's making meaning of the institution, not as deficits.

**MAYA:** And if you're a student affairs practitioner running, say, a residential curriculum?

**DEV:** Three things on Monday morning. First, design your programs to include the validation-experience-mutuality triad. Don't just deliver content; ask participants what they already know, ask them to construct meaning together, validate that they're capable of doing so. Second, expect uneven development across domains, which means a student who is brilliantly self-authored about her career can be in early dualism about her relationships, and your conversation with her has to know which domain you're in. Third, recognize that the highest stages develop after college for most people, which should change what we promise. We are not producing contextual knowers at scale by graduation. We are, at best, opening the door.

**MAYA:** What about faculty?

**DEV:** Faculty should know that the chemistry student we opened with is not a failed student. She's a student in transition, and what feels to her like the professor refusing to do his job is a structural mismatch the professor probably hasn't named for her. Naming it helps. Telling her "you're not crazy, what's happening is that you came in expecting knowledge to behave one way, and the discipline is showing you it behaves another way, and that's hard, and here's why we're doing it anyway" — that's a moment of developmental support. It's also rare.

**MAYA:** And administrators?

**DEV:** Administrators should stop promising learning outcomes the longitudinal data says don't happen in four years. The AAC&U VALUE rubrics for critical thinking implicitly describe contextual or self-authored knowing. The empirical base says most graduating seniors aren't there. That gap between promised outcome and demonstrated outcome is a credibility problem the field has not addressed honestly.

**MAYA:** That's bracing.

**DEV:** Pascarella and Terenzini in *How College Affects Students* are remarkably measured about this. The effect sizes in cognitive development are real but smaller than the marketing copy implies. If you're an administrator making claims about transformation, you should know what the evidence actually shows.

## Reading Roadmap

**DEV:** Four texts, in order. First, Perry's 1970 *Forms of Intellectual and Ethical Development in the College Years*. Read it not because it's empirically current but because it's the clearest articulation of what structural-stage thinking about epistemology even *is*. You need the original to understand what later scholars are revising. Second, Belenky, Clinchy, Goldberger, and Tarule's *Women's Ways of Knowing* from 1986. Read it for the methodological lesson — what changes when you change the sample — and for the categories Perry didn't have access to, particularly silence and connected knowing. Third, King and Kitchener's *Developing Reflective Judgment*, 1994. This gives you the tighter, more psychometric tradition and the Deweyan philosophical anchor; it also shows you what twenty years of longitudinal data looks like. Fourth, Baxter Magolda's *Making Their Own Way*, 2001, paired with Abes, Jones, and McEwen's 2007 reconceptualization piece. Together those give you the contemporary integrative construct and the move into identity work. Read in that order, you get the genealogy in motion rather than as a list.

[pause]

## What We Don't Know

**DEV:** The honest open question is whether structural-stage developmental theory can be done without baked-in hierarchy. Every framework in this tradition names a destination — committed relativism, contextual knowing, reflective judgment, self-authorship — and treats movement toward that destination as development. The decolonial and critical race critiques have shown that those destinations are culturally specific. What nobody has produced is an alternative empirical research program that describes the reorganization of meaning-making without ranking the reorganizations. We don't know whether such a program is possible, or whether the structural-stage method itself requires a teleology to function. If it does, then the whole tradition rests on a normative commitment that needs to be defended in the open, not smuggled in through the vocabulary. That defense has not been written.
