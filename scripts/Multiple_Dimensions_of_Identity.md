# Scholar Stacks — Episode: The Multiple Dimensions of Identity

[COLD OPEN]

DEV: Here's the puzzle. It's 1998. A doctoral student at the University of Maryland sits down with ten women undergraduates and asks them to talk about who they are. One of them is a Black Jewish lesbian from a working-class family. The student development field, at this point, has a Cross model for her Blackness, a D'Augelli model for her lesbian identity, a Josselson model for her womanhood, and basically nothing for her Jewishness or her class. What the field doesn't have is any account of how she's all of those things at the same time, on a Tuesday afternoon, in a sociology seminar where the professor just said something dismissive about welfare queens. Which model do you reach for? You have four. You need one.

MAYA: And that's the gap, right? Because I sit in dissertation prep meetings and people throw around "intersectional lens" like it's a methodological seasoning you sprinkle on. But the actual theoretical apparatus inside student development for holding multiplicity — that's a specific thing built by specific people for a specific reason.

DEV: Susan Jones and Marylu McEwen, 2000, *Journal of College Student Development*. That's the apparatus. And then Elisa Abes joins them in 2007 to rebuild it. And then a whole subsequent literature spends fifteen years arguing about whether what they built was a translation of Kimberlé Crenshaw or a domestication of her.

MAYA: Which is the conversation I actually need to understand, because my committee keeps asking me to "operationalize intersectionality," and I keep wanting to say — operationalize it from where? From Crenshaw's 1989 law review article? From Jones and McEwen? Those are not the same thing.

DEV: They are absolutely not the same thing. So let's do the genealogy properly.

[pause]

[INTELLECTUAL ORIGINS]

DEV: The setup matters. In the late 1990s, student development theory was — I'm going to be a little harsh — it was a field of parallel tracks that didn't talk to each other. You had Chickering and Reisser's seven vectors from the 1993 revision of *Education and Identity*, which treated identity as one vector among seven. You had William Cross's nigrescence model, revised in '91 and '95, doing race. You had Vivienne Cass from 1979 and Anthony D'Augelli from '94 doing sexual orientation. You had Ruthellen Josselson's *Revising Herself* from 1996 doing women's identity through a Marcia-statuses lens. Janet Helms on white racial identity. Jean Phinney on ethnic identity.

MAYA: Each one rigorous on its own axis.

DEV: Each one rigorous on its own axis and each one essentially blind to the others. If you were a researcher studying, say, Latina lesbians at a Catholic university, you had to pick a track. Or you stapled three frameworks together and hoped your dissertation committee didn't notice the seams.

MAYA: Wait — but why was that the dominant move? Why was single-axis the default?

DEV: Two reasons. First, the developmental psychology lineage. Erikson gave the field stages. Chickering imported Erikson into higher education in 1969 and translated stages into vectors. The whole architecture assumed identity was a developmental object you could isolate and track. Second — and this is where the field's class-and-race story matters — the people writing these models were trained in counseling psychology programs that valued internal validity over messy multiplicity. You could publish a clean racial identity scale. You could not, in 1995, publish a clean "how does a Black Jewish lesbian hold herself together" scale.

MAYA: And meanwhile, outside of student development, this was already a solved problem intellectually.

DEV: Solved isn't the word. Theorized. By 1989 Kimberlé Crenshaw had published "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex" in the *University of Chicago Legal Forum*. By 1991 she had "Mapping the Margins" in *Stanford Law Review*. Patricia Hill Collins's *Black Feminist Thought* came out in 1990 with the matrix of domination. Deborah King's "Multiple Jeopardy, Multiple Consciousness" was in *Signs* in 1988. Gloria Anzaldúa's *Borderlands* in '87. All of that was on the shelf.

MAYA: But not on the student affairs shelf.

DEV: Right. Crenshaw was in critical legal studies. Collins was in sociology. Anzaldúa was in Chicana studies and lit. These conversations were not being read by counseling psych faculty advising student affairs dissertations. And here is the political backdrop you have to hold: 1996 is Hopwood, banning race-conscious admissions in Texas. 1996 is Prop 209 in California. 1998 is Initiative 200 in Washington. The Anita Hill hearings in '91 had made the inseparability of race and gender national news. Matthew Shepard was murdered in October '98. So the cultural and legal weather around identity was getting heavier, and the field's theoretical toolkit was getting visibly inadequate.

MAYA: So Jones and McEwen are not making a clever theoretical move. They're answering a practical problem.

DEV: That's how I'd read it. Jones's own dissertation in '97 was qualitative work with women undergraduates, and the women kept refusing to talk about themselves one identity at a time. Listen to how she and McEwen open the 2000 article. They write that prior work assumed a — I'm quoting — "singular nature in which individual identities are constructed and self-perceived," and they argue for examining identity through "the sociocultural and sociopolitical contexts in which identities are constructed." They cite Trickett, Watts, and Birman.

MAYA: That phrase "sociopolitical contexts" is doing real work. That's a gesture toward Crenshaw, even if it doesn't cite her directly.

DEV: It is. And here's the thing — the data forced their hand. They report that for the women they interviewed, "gender was an identity dimension to which they related. However, the description of what being female meant to them was quickly connected with other dimensions — Jewish woman, Black woman, lesbian, Indian woman." The participants would not let them write a single-axis paper.

MAYA: So the model is sort of grounded theory in its bones.

DEV: Grounded in qualitative interview data, yes. Not deduced from Crenshaw. That matters for what happens next.

[pause]

[THE CORE FRAMEWORK]

MAYA: Okay. Walk me through what they actually built.

DEV: Picture an atom. There's a core in the middle — they call it the core sense of self, the personal attributes, the personal identity. Around the core, intersecting rings, like orbital paths. Each ring is a social identity dimension: race, gender, sexual orientation, social class, religion, ability. On each ring there's a dot, and the dot represents the relative salience of that identity at a given moment. Dots closer to the core are more salient; dots further out are less salient. And the whole atom sits inside an outer field they call contextual influences — family background, sociocultural conditions, current experiences, career decisions, life planning.

MAYA: So the picture itself is making three claims. One: there's a core self distinct from social identities. Two: the dimensions are simultaneous, not sequential. Three: salience moves.

DEV: Exactly those three. And let's run a concrete case. Take a student — call her Priya. South Asian American, Hindu, second-generation, middle-class, straight, cisgender woman, sophomore at a predominantly white research university. On Monday morning in a chemistry class where she's one of two women of color and the professor calls on the white men first, her race-and-gender dots are pressed right up against the core. On Tuesday evening at her Hindu Students Association meeting, religion and ethnicity are salient together but in a totally different register — they're sources of belonging, not exclusion. On Wednesday she goes home for a family wedding and class becomes legible in a new way because her cousin who didn't go to college is asking pointed questions about whether her degree is worth the debt.

MAYA: And the model says all three of those are the same identity configuration. What's moving is which dimensions are pressed close to the core and how she experiences them in context.

DEV: That's the original 2000 claim. Now here's what the 2000 model can't explain. Take two Priyas. Same demographic profile, same campus, same chemistry class, same professor calling on white men first. One Priya leaves the class furious and texts her friend group about racial gatekeeping in STEM. The other Priya leaves the class wondering if she's just not smart enough and considers changing her major. Same context, same identities, radically different identity experience.

MAYA: And the 2000 model doesn't have a mechanism for that.

DEV: It doesn't. Which is the problem Elisa Abes's dissertation work on lesbian college students surfaces in the early 2000s. She's finding that students with very similar identity configurations and very similar contexts are processing those contexts in fundamentally different ways. Some students absorb heteronormative messages as truth; others recognize those messages as social products they can hold at a distance. The 2000 model treated contextual influences as acting more or less directly on identity perception. Abes's data said: no, something is happening in between.

MAYA: And that's where Kegan comes in.

DEV: That's where Kegan comes in. Robert Kegan, *The Evolving Self* in 1982, *In Over Our Heads* in 1994. Constructive-developmental psychology. His core insight is that what develops is not the content of our beliefs but our capacity for complexity in meaning-making. He talks about orders of consciousness. What's "subject" — what holds us, what we can't see because we're inside it — versus what's "object" — what we can hold, examine, integrate, choose.

MAYA: And Baxter Magolda translates this for higher ed.

DEV: Marcia Baxter Magolda, *Making Their Own Way*, 2001. She operationalizes Kegan's third-to-fourth order transition as "self-authorship" — the move from external formulas to internal voice. She writes that the deep purpose of higher education is to "steward this transformation so that students and faculty together continually move from naiveté through skepticism to commitment." That's basically Kegan in a higher ed register.

MAYA: So now we get the 2007 piece.

DEV: Abes, Jones, and McEwen, *JCSD*, January/February 2007: "Reconceptualizing the Model of Multiple Dimensions of Identity: The Role of Meaning-Making Capacity in the Construction of Multiple Identities." They take the original atom diagram and insert a filter between the outer ring of contextual influences and the inner atom of identity dimensions. They call it the meaning-making filter.

MAYA: And the filter is calibrated to where the student is developmentally.

DEV: Three positions, drawn from Baxter Magolda. Formulaic meaning-making: minimal filtering, contextual messages pass through more or less as received. Transitional or foundational meaning-making: some filtering. Self-authored: substantial filtering, the student can hold context as object. Listen to how they describe formulaic students — quote: "Because minimal filtering occurs in formulaic meaning making, contextual influences and perceptions of identity are closely connected. Participants infrequently saw relationships between or among their multiple identities. For instance, they perceived their sexual orientation as separate from their ethnicity if that was what they learned from their family; they perceived their sexual orientation as incompatible with their religion if this is how religious leaders taught them to interpret the major teachings of their religion."

MAYA: So the two Priyas — one is filtering and one isn't. The furious one has enough developmental capacity to hold the professor's behavior as object, as a thing she can examine and name. The doubting one is taking it in as content about herself.

DEV: That's the explanatory move. And it's elegant in the sense that it accounts for empirical variance the 2000 model could not. It's also — and this is where the trouble starts — it's a deeply psychological move. The filter is in the head. The locus of development is the individual student's meaning-making capacity.

MAYA: Which is going to matter when we get to the critics.

DEV: It's going to matter a lot.

[pause]

[EVOLUTION AND REFINEMENTS]

MAYA: So between 2007 and now, what's happened to the model?

DEV: Several things. First — and this is the most visible synthesis move — Vasti Torres, Susan Jones, and Kristen Renn co-authored a piece in *JCSD* in 2009 called "Identity Development Theories in Student Affairs," which is essentially a stock-taking of the whole sub-field. They name three waves: foundational (single-axis models), integrative (MMDI and its kin), and critical/poststructural. They argue intersectionality should be understood — and they're quoting Dill and Zambrana here — as a "theoretical intervention," not a theory itself, but "an analytic lens through which theories may be viewed."

MAYA: That's a careful framing.

DEV: It's diplomatically careful. They write that "individual identity exists within and draws from a web of socially defined statuses some of which may be more salient than others in specific situations or at specific historical moments." Notice how that sentence is doing two things at once. It's gesturing toward structural location — "socially defined statuses" — and it's recuperating salience, which is the MMDI's central construct.

MAYA: It's trying to keep the family together.

DEV: It is trying to keep the family together. And they're explicit that intersectionality, in Dill and Zambrana's formulation, requires "placing the lived experiences and struggles of people of color and other marginalized groups as a starting point for the development of theory" and "exploring the complexities not only of individual identities but also group identity... without resorting, even inadvertently, to an additive approach."

MAYA: That's a structural commitment.

DEV: That's a structural commitment that the MMDI, as a model, has a hard time fulfilling. The 2009 essay names the tension without resolving it.

MAYA: So is the original 2000 version still usable? Or do you have to use the 2007 reconceptualization now?

DEV: In practice, almost no one uses the 2000 model without the 2007 filter anymore, because once you've seen the filter, you can't unsee the explanatory gap. But there's a third iteration you should know about — Abes, Jones, and D-L Stewart published an intersectional reconceptualization in 2019, the I-MMDI. That version tries to put structural power back in. Some critics — Lori Patton, Jessica Harris, Chris Linder — consider it an improvement but still insufficient.

MAYA: Why insufficient?

DEV: Because if your theoretical apparatus is fundamentally a developmental psychology apparatus, you can add structural language, but the locus of analysis stays on the individual. You can paint the walls. You can't change the foundation without writing a different building.

MAYA: That's a strong claim.

DEV: It's a claim the critics make. Stay tuned for the live debates section.

[pause]

[LIVE DEBATES]

DEV: Okay, three live arguments. First and most consequential: did the MMDI translate intersectionality or domesticate it? Sirma Bilge published a piece in *Du Bois Review* in 2013 called "Intersectionality Undone: Saving Intersectionality from Feminist Intersectionality Studies." The title tells you the argument. Bilge claims the academy whitened and depoliticized intersectionality — made it a methodology for studying diverse subjects rather than what Crenshaw built, which was an analysis of how interlocking systems of oppression produce particular subjects who fall through the cracks of antidiscrimination law. Lori Patton in *Urban Education* in 2016 makes a parallel argument for higher ed. Jessica Harris and Lori Patton in *Review of Higher Education* in 2019, "Caught in the Crossfire," is the most direct: the field has used "intersectionality" as a buzzword while stripping its anti-subordination commitments.

MAYA: And the charge against the MMDI specifically?

DEV: That the model takes Crenshaw's structural and political concept and renders it as a phenomenology of salience. The question intersectionality is supposed to answer — how do interlocking systems of power produce conditions of subordination — becomes the question "which identity feels most present right now?" Those are not the same question. Crenshaw herself has objected to exactly this kind of psychologization in talks and interviews.

MAYA: What's the defense?

DEV: The defense, which Jones has made publicly, is that the MMDI is doing a different job than Crenshaw was doing. Crenshaw needed a legal argument that would change how courts adjudicated discrimination. Student development needed a developmental account of how individuals experience multiplicity. Different problems, different tools.

MAYA: That feels too tidy to me, Dev. Because the model isn't just sitting in its lane. It's the framework cited in dissertations claiming to do "intersectional analysis." It's the framework taught in master's programs as the field's intersectional model. It's actively occupying the conceptual space.

DEV: That's a fair pushback. And I think the honest answer is: the model has institutional reach that exceeds its theoretical commitments. People use it to do work it wasn't built to do, and the original authors did not loudly correct them.

MAYA: Okay. Second debate?

DEV: Second debate. Is self-authorship culturally universal? Baxter Magolda's program treats internal voice — the move from external formulas to internally generated commitments — as the developmental telos. Indigenous scholars, Asian American scholars, communalist scholars have argued that this valorizes an individuated Western subject. If your culture's developmental ideal is relational interdependence, not internal voice, then "self-authorship" isn't development — it's assimilation dressed up as growth.

MAYA: And that critique attaches to the MMDI because the meaning-making filter is calibrated to self-authorship.

DEV: Exactly. If the filter's developmental endpoint is a culturally specific construction, then the model is rewarding students for becoming Western individualists and reading non-Western relational selves as developmentally arrested.

MAYA: That's a serious charge.

DEV: It's a serious charge. The tradition has responded by adding cultural nuance to interview studies — looking for self-authorship in culturally varied registers — without revising the core developmental telos. Whether that's enough is contested.

MAYA: Third debate?

DEV: Third: where does the analytic burden sit? On the student or on the institution? Samuel Museus and Uma Jayakumar's 2012 *Creating Campus Cultures*, and Museus's CECE model in 2017, argue that the identity development tradition — including MMDI — places the analytic burden on the student. The student is the figure; the racialized environment is ground. The student is expected to develop the meaning-making capacity to handle a hostile climate, while the hostile climate goes untheorized.

MAYA: But the 2000 model has contextual influences in the outer ring. That's the environment.

DEV: It has context as influence on the student. It does not have institutional structure as a unit of analysis in its own right. The model can ask "how is this student processing the campus climate?" It cannot ask "what is the campus climate doing, structurally, to produce this student's experience?" Those require different analytic moves.

MAYA: So where do you land?

DEV: I think the MMDI is a careful piece of work that is being asked to do too much. It's a useful developmental description. It is not an intersectional framework in Crenshaw's sense, and I think we'd all be better off if the field stopped calling it one. Use it as what it is — a constructivist developmental account of how individuals experience multiplicity — and pair it with structural analysis from elsewhere when structure is what you need.

MAYA: I want to push back. You're being generous in a way I'm not sure the model deserves. Because if the field doesn't have anything that does the intersectional work, and the MMDI is occupying that space, then "use it as what it is" is sort of letting it off the hook for crowding out the work that should be there.

DEV: That's the strongest version of the critique. I'll concede it. The model's institutional position has costs that aren't visible inside the model itself.

[pause]

[WHAT THE FIELD GETS WRONG]

DEV: Here's the most common misreading I see in dissertations. People treat the MMDI as if "intersection" in the model means what "intersection" means in Crenshaw. It doesn't. In the model, intersection is a visual metaphor for dimensions co-occurring around a core. In Crenshaw, intersection is a metaphor for traffic — for the specific harm produced when multiple systems of oppression converge in ways the law refuses to see. The model's rings are descriptive; Crenshaw's intersection is analytic and political.

MAYA: So when a student affairs practitioner says "I'm doing intersectional programming using the MMDI" — what's wrong with that?

DEV: What's usually wrong is that they're doing programming that invites students to share their multiple identities, validate one another's complexity, and develop their meaning-making capacity. Which is fine work. It just isn't intersectional work in the Crenshaw sense, because it isn't naming or contesting the interlocking systems that produce subordination. Calling it intersectional inflates what's happening and lets the institution off the hook for the structural work.

MAYA: The other thing I see is people using "salience" as if it's a measurement.

DEV: Yes. Salience in the model is contextual and phenomenological — what feels present in this moment. It is not a stable trait you can score a student on. When you see survey instruments asking "rank your identities by importance," that is a misreading of the model. The whole point of salience is that it shifts.

[pause]

[IMPLICATIONS FOR YOUR WORK]

MAYA: All right, I want to land this in practice. Let me try to translate this for the people listening.

If you are a doctoral student writing a dissertation that wants to do intersectional work — pick your apparatus deliberately. Do not cite Jones and McEwen, Crenshaw, and Collins in the same paragraph as if they're saying the same thing. They are not. If your research question is about how individual students experience and integrate multiple identities developmentally, the MMDI and the 2007 reconceptualization are good tools. If your research question is about how interlocking structures of power produce particular conditions of subordination, you need Crenshaw, Collins, Bilge, Patton, and methodologically you probably need critical race methodology, not constructivist interviewing.

DEV: And if you're doing both, be explicit about the seam. Don't pretend it isn't there.

MAYA: For student affairs practitioners — I think the practical move is to stop using the MMDI as cover for not doing structural work. If you're running identity development workshops, name what you're doing. You're supporting students in developing meaning-making capacity around multiple identities. That is valuable. It is not the same as changing the institutional conditions that make those identities differentially costly to hold.

DEV: One more practitioner implication. The 2007 filter is genuinely useful for advising. Two students in the same protest, same campus, same identity profile, can be in completely different developmental positions. The one who's furious and organizing has self-authored capacity to hold the institution as object. The one who's spiraling and questioning their belonging is processing institutional messages as truth about themselves. They need different support. The model gives you language for that distinction without pathologizing either student.

MAYA: For faculty — and this one is sharper — if you teach this model, teach the critique. Don't teach the 2000 model and the 2007 model and stop. Assign Bilge. Assign Harris and Patton. Assign the I-MMDI from 2019. Let students see that the field is in an ongoing argument about whether its central intersectional framework is intersectional. That is the actual intellectual content of the field right now, and hiding it from students produces credentialed practitioners who can't think structurally.

DEV: For administrators — and this is the one I want to underline given Students for Fair Admissions and the state DEI bans — if the only theoretical apparatus your DEI infrastructure has for identity work is a developmental phenomenology, you cannot defend the institutional structures that work is meant to support. You need a theory that can name white supremacy, capitalism, heteropatriarchy as structures, because the political opposition has theories that name "diversity" as ideology. You can't fight a structural argument with a developmental one.

MAYA: That's the stakes.

[pause]

[READING ROADMAP]

DEV: Four texts, in order, for a doctoral student encountering this concept.

First: Jones and McEwen, 2000, in *JCSD*. Read the original article. Look at the figure. Read the participants' words. Notice how grounded the model is in qualitative data and how cautiously it gestures toward sociopolitical context without theorizing it. This is your baseline. You can't critique what you don't first understand on its own terms.

Second: Abes, Jones, and McEwen, 2007, in *JCSD*. Read it next because you need to see how the same authors revised their own work in response to empirical insufficiency. It's a model of intellectual honesty in published self-revision. And it shows you what the Kegan-Baxter Magolda turn does to the framework — what it explains and what it obscures.

Third: Torres, Jones, and Renn, 2009, in *JCSD*. This is the field looking at itself. Read it to see how three central figures map the pluralism — foundational, integrative, critical — and where they place intersectionality in that map. Notice what they admit and what they hold in tension.

Fourth — and this is the one outside the corpus but essential — Harris and Patton, 2019, "Caught in the Crossfire," in *Review of Higher Education*. This is the reckoning. Read it last because you need the first three to understand what Harris and Patton are arguing against. Without the foundation, the critique sounds like polemic. With the foundation, it sounds like the field finally saying something true out loud.

[pause]

[WHAT WE DON'T KNOW]

DEV: Here's the open question. We do not have a developmental account of how students come to read structure. Not how they experience their own identities — we have that. Not how they make meaning of context — we have that. But how does a student move from experiencing racism as a series of interpersonal events to recognizing racism as a structure, a system, a political economy? What does that developmental sequence look like? What pedagogies support it? What gets in the way?

MAYA: Because if the critique of the MMDI is that it's a developmental model without a structural register, the response can't only be "add structure to the model." It has to be: build a developmental account of structural consciousness itself.

DEV: And that account does not exist in the field. There are pieces of it scattered in critical race pedagogy, in Paolo Freire's conscientização, in some of the Black feminist literature on consciousness. But there is no integrative developmental theory of how undergraduates come to see structure as structure. We have models of how they come to see themselves. We do not have models of how they come to see the systems that produce them.

MAYA: Which is the most important question the field hasn't answered.

DEV: It might be the question the next generation of this scholarship has to answer if any of this work is going to survive the political pressure it's now under.
