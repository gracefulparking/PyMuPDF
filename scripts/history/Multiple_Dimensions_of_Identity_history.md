# Historical Context Briefing: Multiple Dimensions of Identity
*Literature window: 2000–present*

---

═══════════════════════════════════════════════
CONTEXT BRIEFING: MULTIPLE DIMENSIONS OF IDENTITY (MDI)
Susan Jones & Marylu McEwen, 2000 → Abes, Jones, McEwen, 2007 → present
═══════════════════════════════════════════════

A note before we start: MDI is a student development model, which means it sits at the intersection of counseling psychology, college student personnel work, and — increasingly after 2007 — critical theory. Understanding why Jones and McEwen wrote what they wrote in 2000 requires understanding both what the field of student development looked like at that moment (dominated by single-axis stage models inherited from the 1970s and 80s) and what was happening in legal scholarship and ethnic studies that they were trying, with mixed success, to import. Keep that double genealogy in mind.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════
ERA 1: THE PRE-HISTORY AND IMMEDIATE ANTECEDENTS (1987–1999)
═══════════════════════════════════════════════

This era matters because Jones and McEwen's 2000 model was a response to a very specific theoretical landscape. You can't understand the contribution without understanding what they were pushing against.

▸ HIGHER ED POLICY LANDSCAPE

— Regents of the University of California v. Bakke (1978) was still the operative affirmative action precedent. Diversity as a "compelling interest" framing — Justice Powell's opinion — had become the legal justification for race-conscious admissions, which pushed institutions toward "diversity" as an educational rationale rather than a remedial one.
— Hopwood v. Texas (5th Circuit, 1996) banned race-conscious admissions in TX, LA, MS — creating real institutional anxiety about how to defend diversity work.
— California Proposition 209 (1996) and Washington Initiative 200 (1998) banned affirmative action in those states.
— The 1998 reauthorization of the Higher Education Act expanded TRIO and GEAR UP, signaling federal interest in access for underrepresented students.

▸ STUDENT DEMOGRAPHIC SHIFTS

— Undergraduate enrollment grew from ~12.5 million (1987) to ~14.8 million (1999) per NCES Digest of Education Statistics.
— Women became a clear majority of undergraduates during this period (crossing 55% by the late 1990s).
— Students of color grew from ~17% to ~28% of undergraduate enrollment over the decade [verify exact figures, but the trajectory is well-established].
— The "traditional" 18–22-year-old residential undergraduate was already a minority of the actual student body by the mid-1990s — a fact that complicated the largely residential, traditional-age samples used in student development research.

▸ INSTITUTIONAL CONDITIONS

— Student affairs was professionalizing rapidly: ACPA and NASPA were producing standards (the 1987 Perspective on Student Affairs and the 1996 Student Learning Imperative), and master's programs in college student personnel/higher ed were expanding.
— State appropriations per FTE began their long decline; this is the start of the tuition-dependency curve that defines the next two decades.
— Multicultural affairs offices, LGBT resource centers (the first was at U Michigan, 1971; significant expansion in the 1990s), and women's centers were proliferating — creating an institutional client base for identity-focused scholarship.

▸ ADJACENT INTELLECTUAL MOVEMENTS

This is the crucial section. Jones & McEwen were responding to:

— Single-axis racial identity models: William Cross's Nigrescence model (1971, revised 1991, 1995); Jean Phinney's ethnic identity model (1989, 1992); Janet Helms's White Racial Identity Development (1990).
— Sexual identity models: Vivienne Cass (1979), Anthony D'Augelli (1994).
— Women's identity: Ruthellen Josselson, Revising Herself (1996), drawing on Marcia's identity statuses.
— The dominant umbrella model: Arthur Chickering's Education and Identity (1969, revised with Linda Reisser 1993), which treated identity as one of seven vectors but did not theorize multiplicity.

And — critically — outside student development:

— Kimberlé Crenshaw, "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex" (1989, U Chicago Legal Forum) and "Mapping the Margins" (Stanford Law Review, 1991). These are the foundational intersectionality texts, written in critical race legal theory.
— Patricia Hill Collins, Black Feminist Thought (1990) — "matrix of domination."
— Deborah King, "Multiple Jeopardy, Multiple Consciousness" (Signs, 1988).
— Gloria Anzaldúa, Borderlands/La Frontera (1987).
— Robert Kegan, The Evolving Self (1982) and In Over Our Heads (1994) — constructive-developmental theory. Kegan would later become central to the 2007 reconceptualization.

The intellectual gap Jones & McEwen identified: student development had single-axis models for race, for gender, for sexual orientation — but no theoretical apparatus for how a student holds these simultaneously. Crenshaw and Collins had the apparatus, but it lived in law and sociology, not in developmental psychology.

▸ POLITICAL AND CULTURAL CLIMATE

— The "culture wars" of the early 1990s: Allan Bloom's Closing of the American Mind (1987), Dinesh D'Souza's Illiberal Education (1991), and the "political correctness" backlash framed identity-conscious scholarship as suspect.
— The 1991 Anita Hill/Clarence Thomas hearings did enormous work in popular consciousness around the inseparability of race and gender — Crenshaw was writing directly into this moment.
— Don't Ask Don't Tell (1993); Defense of Marriage Act (1996). LGBT identity was politically contested in ways that shaped what student affairs felt it could and couldn't do.
— The Matthew Shepard murder (October 1998) made campus climate for LGBT students a national conversation.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════
ERA 2: THE ORIGINAL MODEL AND ITS RECEPTION (2000–2006)
═══════════════════════════════════════════════

▸ HIGHER ED POLICY LANDSCAPE

— Grutter v. Bollinger and Gratz v. Bollinger (2003) — the Michigan cases. Grutter affirmed diversity as a compelling interest (re-validating Bakke's logic); Gratz struck down the points-based undergraduate admissions system. This created an enormous demand for diversity research that could justify educational benefits — institutional money flowed into this area.
— No Child Left Behind (2002) — primarily K-12, but it normalized large-scale outcomes assessment that would migrate to higher ed.
— Spellings Commission (A Test of Leadership, 2006) — pushed accountability and outcomes framing.

▸ STUDENT DEMOGRAPHIC SHIFTS

— Total undergraduate enrollment grew from ~13.2M (2000) to ~17.5M (2006) — one of the steepest growth periods in higher ed history, driven by both demographic echo and recession-era enrollment.
— Latinx enrollment grew especially rapidly; Hispanic-Serving Institution designations expanded significantly.
— First-generation college students became an explicit policy and research category.

▸ INSTITUTIONAL CONDITIONS

— Post-Grutter, "Chief Diversity Officer" positions began proliferating at major universities.
— State disinvestment accelerated after the 2001 recession.
— ACPA's "Principles of Good Practice for Student Affairs" (joint with NASPA) and Learning Reconsidered (2004) reframed student affairs as a learning enterprise — opening space for theory-rich identity work as evidence of learning.

▸ ADJACENT INTELLECTUAL MOVEMENTS

— Marcia Baxter Magolda's Making Their Own Way (2001) brought Kegan's constructive-developmental framework into student development with the concept of "self-authorship." This is the proximate cause of the 2007 reconceptualization — Baxter Magolda's work made Kegan legible to student development scholars.
— Critical race theory in education: Daniel Solórzano, Tara Yosso (e.g., "Critical Race Methodology," 2002).
— Queer theory entering education: Susan Talburt, Shirley Steinberg (eds.), Thinking Queer (2000).
— D-L Stewart, Chris Linder, and others began publishing on identity intersections at HBCUs and predominantly white institutions.

▸ POLITICAL AND CULTURAL CLIMATE

— 9/11 and its aftermath made Muslim and Arab American student identity newly visible and surveilled — religion as a salient identity category gained urgency that the original 2000 model had treated as one dimension among others.
— Massachusetts legalized same-sex marriage (2004); the 2004 election made same-sex marriage a wedge issue in 11 state ballots.
— Hurricane Katrina (2005) — race, class, and place collided publicly in ways that reinforced intersectional analysis as common sense.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════
ERA 3: THE RECONCEPTUALIZED MODEL AND ITS DIFFUSION (2007–2014)
═══════════════════════════════════════════════

▸ HIGHER ED POLICY LANDSCAPE

— Parents Involved in Community Schools v. Seattle (2007) — K-12, but signaled judicial hostility to race-conscious policy that affected higher ed thinking.
— Fisher v. University of Texas I (2013) — sent Grutter framework back for stricter scrutiny.
— Obama administration "Dear Colleague" letter on Title IX and sexual violence (April 4, 2011) — fundamentally reshaped campus practice around gender and sexual violence.
— Postsecondary Education Opportunity Act expansions; Pell Grant funding fluctuations during the recession.

▸ STUDENT DEMOGRAPHIC SHIFTS

— Total enrollment peaked at ~21 million (2010–2011) during the Great Recession, then began a decline.
— Community college enrollment surged then receded.
— For-profit enrollment exploded then crashed (Corinthian Colleges collapse begins 2014).
— Students of color became roughly 40% of undergraduate enrollment.
— Undocumented students became visible as a population — DACA announced June 15, 2012.

▸ INSTITUTIONAL CONDITIONS

— The Great Recession (2008–2009) accelerated state disinvestment dramatically — many states cut higher ed appropriations 20–30%.
— Tuition shifted from supplementing to surpassing state appropriations at most publics.
— Student affairs assessment infrastructure expanded (Council for the Advancement of Standards, EBI/Skyfactor surveys).

▸ ADJACENT INTELLECTUAL MOVEMENTS

— Abes, Jones, & McEwen's 2007 article in the Journal of College Student Development ("Reconceptualizing the Model of Multiple Dimensions of Identity") integrated Kegan's meaning-making as the filter between context and identity salience. This is the move that made MDI a constructivist-developmental model rather than a structural one.
— Patricia Hill Collins and Sirma Bilge would later publish Intersectionality (2016), but the broader uptake of "intersectionality" in education accelerated in this window — and with it, the first round of critiques that student development scholars had domesticated Crenshaw.
— Critical disability studies entered student affairs (Annemarie Vaccaro and colleagues, mid-2010s).
— Chela Sandoval, Methodology of the Oppressed (2000), and other women-of-color feminist theorists became more widely cited.
— Stephen Quaye and Shaun Harper's edited volume Student Engagement in Higher Education (2009; 2nd ed 2015) helped institutionalize an identity-conscious student engagement framework.

▸ POLITICAL AND CULTURAL CLIMATE

— Obama's election (2008) and the contested "post-racial" framing.
— Occupy Wall Street (2011) made class salience newly conversational on campuses.
— Trayvon Martin killed February 2012; George Zimmerman acquitted July 2013; #BlackLivesMatter coined July 2013.
— Michael Brown killed August 9, 2014; the Ferguson uprisings followed.
— Repeal of Don't Ask Don't Tell (2011); Windsor (2013) struck down DOMA Section 3.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════
ERA 4: CRITIQUE, EXTENSION, AND CONTESTED INHERITANCE (2015–present)
═══════════════════════════════════════════════

▸ HIGHER ED POLICY LANDSCAPE

— Fisher v. UT II (2016) — narrowly upheld Grutter framework one more time.
— Title IX rule changes under DeVos (2020) and revisions under Cardona (2024) — gender identity inclusion contested in courts.
— Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard / v. UNC (June 2023) — effectively ended race-conscious admissions. This is the most significant legal shift in the model's lifetime.
— State-level legislation restricting "divisive concepts" and DEI offices: Florida SB 266 (2023), Texas SB 17 (2023), and similar in roughly a dozen states by 2024. These directly affect the institutional infrastructure that uses MDI.

▸ STUDENT DEMOGRAPHIC SHIFTS

— The "demographic cliff" — declining number of 18-year-olds beginning ~2025 — drove enrollment anxiety.
— Undergraduate enrollment declined from ~17M (2014) to ~15.4M (2022).
— Men's enrollment declined further; the gender gap widened.
— Students reporting LGBTQ+ identification rose sharply in survey data (Gallup, Higher Education Research Institute CIRP).
— Mental health emerged as a dominant student affairs concern — anxiety and depression reports roughly doubled in HERI/Healthy Minds data over the decade.

▸ INSTITUTIONAL CONDITIONS

— Small private college closures accelerated.
— Public flagship tuition surpassed state appropriation share at most universities.
— DEI office expansion (2015–2020) followed by DEI office contraction or elimination in red states (2023–2024).
— Contingent faculty became the majority of instructional staff.

▸ ADJACENT INTELLECTUAL MOVEMENTS

— QuantCrit (Nichole Garcia, Nancy López, Verónica Vélez and colleagues, mid-2010s) brought critical race theory into quantitative methods.
— Critiques of the developmental translation of intersectionality intensified: see Stephen John Quaye, Chris Linder, and others arguing that MDI and similar frameworks risk psychologizing what Crenshaw posed as structural.
— Jessica Harris and Lori Patton's "Caught in the Crossfire: Tensions Surrounding Intersectionality in Higher Education" (2019, Review of Higher Education) is a key piece — argues the field has used "intersectionality" as a buzzword while stripping its anti-subordination commitments.
— Susan Jones herself has engaged this critique; the Jones & Abes textbook Identity Development of College Students (2013) and subsequent work reflects the dialogue.

▸ POLITICAL AND CULTURAL CLIMATE

— 2016 election and a wave of campus protests over racism, Title IX, immigration, and free speech.
— #MeToo (October 2017).
— George Floyd murder (May 25, 2020); the racial reckoning summer.
— Dobbs v. Jackson (June 2022) — reproductive rights re-entered campus political consciousness.
— Post-October 2023 campus conflicts over Israel/Gaza tested intersectional analysis under acute political pressure.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════
TRANSITIONS BETWEEN ERAS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════

ERA 1 → ERA 2 (the original 2000 model): The decisive transition was the accumulating critique within identity development circles that single-axis models couldn't account for what scholars and practitioners were actually hearing from students. Jones's own qualitative dissertation work (1997) on women college students had foregrounded multiplicity. Crenshaw and Collins's frameworks had reached education through the mid-1990s but lacked a developmental translation. Jones and McEwen, both at University of Maryland in a strong student affairs program, did that translation — drawing the now-iconic atom diagram of a core self with intersecting identity rings whose salience varies by context.

ERA 2 → ERA 3 (the 2007 reconceptualization): Baxter Magolda's work in the early 2000s made Kegan's constructive-developmental theory legible to student development scholars in a way it hadn't been before. Elisa Abes's dissertation work on lesbian college students surfaced the problem that students at different meaning-making positions experienced "the same" contextual influences (e.g., heteronormative campus climate) very differently. Adding meaning-making capacity as a filter solved an empirical puzzle the 2000 model couldn't handle. It also — and this is the contested move — pulled the model further from Crenshaw's structural register and deeper into psychological constructivism.

ERA 3 → ERA 4 (critique era): Three forces converged. First, the post-Ferguson period made many junior scholars of color impatient with frameworks that treated structural racism as a "contextual influence" filtered through individual meaning-making. Second, the broader academic conversation about intersectionality as a buzzword (Sirma Bilge's "Intersectionality Undone," 2013, Du Bois Review) reached education. Third, the SFFA decision and state DEI bans made the political stakes of "depoliticized" diversity frameworks newly visible — if your theory of identity can't name white supremacy as a structure, it cannot defend the institutional work it has been used to justify.

KEY FUNDING AND INSTITUTIONAL ACTORS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════

— Journals: Journal of College Student Development (the gatekeeper for this literature — published both the 2000 and 2007 articles); Review of Higher Education; Journal of Higher Education; Journal of Diversity in Higher Education (founded 2008); Journal of Student Affairs Research and Practice.
— Professional associations: ACPA (College Student Educators International) and NASPA (Student Affairs Administrators in Higher Education) — ACPA's Commission for Professional Preparation and standing committees on race, gender, sexuality were institutional homes for this work. ASHE (Association for the Study of Higher Education) for the more scholarly wing. AERA Division J (Postsecondary Education).
— Doctoral programs: University of Maryland (Jones, McEwen, Komives), Ohio State (Abes), Iowa State, Penn State, Indiana, Michigan State, Maryland — these student affairs/higher ed PhD programs trained the scholars who extended MDI.
— Foundations: The funding history here is somewhat thin. Spencer Foundation funded some student development research; Lumina and Gates focused more on completion and access than identity per se. Much MDI-derivative scholarship was unfunded dissertation work or supported by internal university grants. This is itself worth noting — the work was institutionally cheap to produce, which is part of why it proliferated rapidly.
— Federal: NCES enrollment data underwrites all demographic claims; IES funded some related work but not MDI directly [verify]. Postsecondary research grants from OPE supported some adjacent work.

═══════════════════════════════════════════════
INTELLECTUAL FEUDS, DEBATES, AND TURNING POINTS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════

— Jones & McEwen (2000, JCSD) vs. the single-axis tradition: the original article explicitly positioned itself against Chickering/Reisser's vector model and against using race-only or gender-only frameworks.
— Abes, Jones, & McEwen (2007, JCSD): not a feud but a self-revision — a rare and intellectually generous public reworking of one's own model.
— Sirma Bilge, "Intersectionality Undone: Saving Intersectionality from Feminist Intersectionality Studies" (Du Bois Review, 2013) — the foundational critique that intersectionality had been "whitened" and depoliticized in academic uptake. Education scholars including Lori Patton picked this up.
— Lori Patton, "Disrupting Postsecondary Prose: Toward a Critical Race Theory of Higher Education" (Urban Education, 2016) — argued the field had inadequately engaged structural racism in identity scholarship.
— Jessica Harris & Lori Patton, "Caught in the Crossfire" (RHE, 2019) — the most direct critique of how higher ed scholarship has deployed intersectionality, including in models like MDI.
— Susan Jones's own response, including in Identity Development of College Students (with Abes, 2013) and subsequent essays, where she acknowledges the depoliticization critique while defending the developmental translation as serving a different purpose. The exchange is generative rather than acrimonious.
— Stephen Quaye, Chris Linder, and others on whether developmental models can hold structural critique without contradiction.
— Adjacent debate: Baxter Magolda's self-authorship vs. critical scholars who argue self-authorship valorizes individuation as the developmental telos in ways that are culturally specific.
