# **A Review of*****In Over Our Heads: The Mental Demands***
# ***of Modern Life***

## **Debra A. Harkins****1****,****2**

What do we, as teachers, managers, policymakers,
therapists, institutional leaders, curriculum designers,
intellectual historians, educational philosophers, and
philosophers of mind expect others to learn and
know? What is the "hidden curriculum" of our rap­
idly evolving and highly complex postindustrial soci­
ety? Robert Kegan, author of the widely acclaimed
_The Evolving Self,_has, in his new book _In over Our_
_Heads: The Mental Demands of Modem Life_(Kegan,
1994), uncovered the many tasks and expectations of
modern life and found them to be incompatible with
most adults' ways of knowing the world. He also ex­
plores the implications of this incompatibility for adults
who partner, parent, employ, heal, or teach. This he
does with great clarity, success, and compassion.
Kegan uses the metaphors of "curriculum" and
"school" to explore the many demands that culture
imposes on adults and the relation of those demands
to the development of adult human consciousness.
He begins by revealing that adults unknowingly ex­
pect adolescents to develop an order of conscious­
ness required for participation in a traditional world.
This order of consciousness, claims Kegan, was all
that was needed for adults in preindustrial society.
However, the demands of modem life are more com­
plex and adults are expected to juggle the responsi­
bilities and demands that arise in their partnering,
parenting, working, learning, and healing relation­
ships. These demands of adult modern life, again un­
knowingly, require a higher order of consciousness
than most adults currently possess. Although seem­
ingly disparate demands are being called for in these
noncommunicating disciplines of life, Kegan demon-

strates, through striking examples, that there are re­
markable similarities to the complexity of mind being
asked of adults. Taken together, these cultural tasks
constitute an order of consciousness that is at the
threshold of modernity.
What are these mental demands of modern life
and how many adults know them? Kegan asserts that
adults are expected to be the authors, creators, and
organizers of their thoughts, feelings, roles, and re­
lationships yet less than half of adults who reach tra­
ditional meaning-making attain a modem order of
consciousness. If one recalls Piaget's disturbing state­
ment that less than half of adults reach formal op­
erational reasoning, which is analogous to traditional
meaning-making, then Kegan is actually postulating
that only one-fourth of all adults attain this modern
order of consciousness.
More specifically, these mental demands require
adults to be responsible for, rather than to, their
roles. Using many examples from everyday life as
well as from his own research, Kegan demonstrates
that many adults are aware of shared feelings, agree­
ments, and expectations, but have no generaliz1/d sys­
tem for the regulation of those shared feelings,
agreements, and expectations. Further, many adults
can reason abstractly as well as reason about reason­
ing, but cannot produce all the combinations of re­
lationships or isolate the variables to test their
hypotheses, a task demand of modern life. Intraper­
sonally, many adults can internalize another's point
of view and empathize with another, but cannot or­
ganize these internalizations or empathies into a co­
herent self.

human consciousness involves a process in which
what was initially subject becomes the object of self­
reflection. Each developmental order of conscious­
